# Reviewer A

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes a frame-level monitor that estimates an interaction preference value (IPV), abstains when the estimate or reference is unsupported, and compares readable values with situation-conditioned conformal ranges learned from human trajectories. It further applies the monitor to naturalistic datasets, 19 automated-driving systems in 15 closed-course scenarios, and 20 human drivers in the same scenario set. The paper is unusually explicit about abstention, marginal coverage, source shift and the distinction between atypicality and harm.

However, the evidence establishes model-relative trajectory atypicality, not social compliance. IPV is neither independently validated as perceived social preference nor shown to outperform simpler kinematic or behavioural-deviation measures. The consequence analysis is temporally endogenous to the trajectory window generating the flag, relies on heavily repeated moment-level observations, and does not support the interpretation that flagged interactions become less benign. The central transfer claim is also weakened by poor leave-one-source-out coverage, a low benchmark verdict rate, and the absence of uncertainty and a complete design analysis for the human arm. These are not presentational defects: they affect construct validity, statistical validity and the main claim. Substantial new validation and reanalysis would be required for an NMI-level case.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Abstract; Introduction; Sections 2.1, 2.3 and 3; Methods 4.1; Figs. 1–2] — “Social compliance” is not validated as the construct being measured.** The IPV is a parameter of the authors' inverse-planning model: seven candidate values trade an own-progress term against an interaction term. Concentration of candidate likelihoods shows only that this model distinguishes its own candidates. Agreement between a causal, map-referenced estimate and an offline estimate generated under the same modelling assumptions is likewise estimator self-consistency, not evidence that the estimate recovers a human social preference. No human observer, interacting driver, independent behavioural instrument, controlled preference manipulation or external social-norm annotation validates either the direction or magnitude of IPV. This matters because the title and broad contribution are about socially compliant autonomous driving, whereas the demonstrated object is departure from a model-derived human trajectory distribution. The careful statement that atypicality is not inappropriateness is scientifically correct, but it also removes the evidence needed to support the paper's headline construct. The remedy is independent convergent and discriminant validation: blinded ratings from affected road users and external observers; controlled scenarios that manipulate courteous/assertive intent while holding physical risk and efficiency approximately fixed; comparison with independently specified SVO, courtesy, yielding and surprise measures; and tests showing that IPV predicts these measurements beyond elementary kinematics. Without such evidence, the paper should be retitled and framed throughout as monitoring conditional interaction-trajectory atypicality.

2. **[Methods 4.1–4.4; Sections 2.1 and 2.3; Figs. 2 and 4] — The verdict is highly model-specific, yet estimator uncertainty and specification sensitivity are not propagated into the monitor.** The seven-point grid, one-second window, Gaussian position likelihood with \(\sigma=0.1\) m, static lane-centre reference, utility definition and maximum-weight threshold of 0.20 jointly determine the reading. The “reliability” gate is not calibrated uncertainty on \(\hat\theta\); Fig. 2b in fact shows frame-to-frame movement of about 0.30 rad when the estimate is most readable. The conformal interval then calibrates variation of these point estimates, not uncertainty in the latent preference or in the inverse-planning model. A planner or driver whose dynamics are poorly represented by the candidate generator can therefore be flagged because of model mismatch rather than social behaviour. This directly challenges the claimed planner-agnostic character. The paper needs recovery experiments with known generative preferences, sensitivity to candidate grid, cost weights, \(\sigma\), window length, map/reference error and observation noise, and replication with substantively different inverse-planning or behavioural models. Measurement uncertainty should be carried through to range membership, and verdict stability/flicker should be reported. The full cost functions and solver details must also be present or archived in a reviewable artifact, not deferred to a future code release.

3. **[Sections 2.4 and 2.6; Methods 4.5; Fig. 5; Fig. ED2] — The “consequence” analysis does not separate a flag from interaction dynamics already under way.** A flag uses a one-second trajectory history ending at time \(t\), and the outcome window begins at that same \(t\). Successive flagged moments are autocorrelated and their post-verdict windows overlap. Relative kinematics and risk proxies also enter the conditioning state, while the inverse planner observes the counterpart's trajectory. Fig. ED2 visibly shows the counterpart decelerating before the displayed flagged stretch. Thus, greater subsequent speed reduction or a smaller margin may simply be continuation of a pre-existing interaction state that helped generate the flag. Reassigning entire flag sequences across runs does not eliminate within-run time-varying confounding or regression to the mean. The phrases “followed by” and “compression of ordinary interaction quality” therefore exceed the design, even if the authors avoid a formal causal claim. Remedy this with onset-based, non-overlapping event analyses; a pre-specified temporal guard gap; pre-trend and change-from-baseline outcomes; matching or weighting on the full pre-flag trajectory; one exposure unit per independent alert episode; negative-control times and outcomes; and scenario/system-stratified comparisons. A prospective intervention or observer/driver-response study would be needed before interpreting the association as an interaction burden.

4. **[Sections 2.4–2.6; Methods 4.5–4.6; Figs. 5–6] — The inferential unit and crossed dependence structure are inadequate for the headline comparisons.** The large displayed \(n\)'s are moments, but the benchmark contains only 15 scenario templates, 19 systems and 267 runs; 472 assertive-side moments used in Fig. 5a–b cluster in 120 runs. The human arm is a 20-driver × 15-scenario repeated-measures design. Resampling 175 runs accounts for within-run moment dependence but does not by itself support generalisation across a crossed set of scenario templates, automated systems and human participants. Overlapping outcome windows further reduce effective information. Figure 6 reports the central 2.1-fold comparison and “15/15 scenarios” without uncertainty, while the several endpoints and thresholds in Fig. 5 use pointwise intervals. The remedy is a hierarchical analysis with scenario, system and driver effects (and their relevant interactions), alert-episode rather than frame-level units, cluster-robust or crossed bootstrap inference, and multiplicity-aware simultaneous intervals for the frozen endpoint family. Report run-, system-, driver- and scenario-level distributions, not only pooled moment rates. Explain why 19 × 15 yields 267 rather than 285 runs and account for all missing/excluded runs before analysis.

5. **[Sections 2.3 and 2.5; Methods 4.3–4.4; Figs. 3c, 4 and 6] — Calibration under distribution shift is not established, and abstention selects a narrow, potentially easy target-domain subset.** The guarantee is only pointwise marginal coverage over accepted moments. Leave-one-source-out 90% coverage falls to 0.743 for Waymo and 0.750 for Lyft, with negative held-out \(R^2\) for two sources; this is strong evidence that the conditional model does not transfer reliably across even the four constituent corpora. On the real-vehicle benchmark, only 14,099 of 67,861 candidate moments (20.8%) receive a verdict, and 36 of 267 runs receive none because of human-support limitations. On the matched human course, the 90% outside rate is 4.7%, not the nominal approximately 10%. A lower alarm rate proves only “no inflation”; it does not show that the interval remains calibrated or informative after the move, and it may indicate excessive width or selective acceptance. Consequently, the assertion that the instrument “survives the move” across country, apparatus and task is too strong. The paper needs target-domain coverage with uncertainty, two-sided calibration/equivalence criteria, coverage-versus-abstention curves, conditional coverage diagnostics by scenario, role, geometry, risk and interaction phase, and full reason-specific abstention rates for humans and each automated system. Any comparison of flag rates must standardise the accepted state distribution; otherwise differing support/readability mixtures can generate the human–AV contrast.

6. **[Section 2.5; Methods 4.6; Fig. 6] — The human-reference experiment is insufficiently specified and does not isolate a machine effect.** The manuscript gives only “20 licensed drivers × 15 scenarios” and common scripted injections. It does not report recruitment, demographics and driving experience, country/licensing background, ethics approval and consent, compensation, safety oversight, scenario order/counterbalancing, practice, repeated exposure, missing trials, or whether analysts were blinded. These factors are especially important in deliberately staged conflicts on a closed course. Moreover, pooled judgeable moments are not a matched denominator: humans and automated systems may reach different states, have different interaction durations and pass the gates at different rates. A 2.1-fold moment-level flag ratio can therefore reflect exposure composition, repeated-scenario learning, or the unusually conservative behaviour of the course drivers rather than a difference between humans and machines. The authors should provide the complete protocol and ethics statement; report participant- and scenario-level estimates with crossed uncertainty; compare humans and systems within common-support, scenario-time or matched-state strata; separate assertive and over-yielding alarms; and pre-specify an inferential estimand for the human–AV comparison. The “same population that defined the reference” wording should be removed: these are different drivers in a different sampling context.

7. **[Introduction; Sections 2.4–2.6 and 3; Fig. 5] — The observed endpoints do not establish that the flagged state is socially worse, and some evidence points in the opposite safety direction.** Assertive-side flags are associated with lower median/upper-quartile ego margins and greater counterpart speed adjustment, but emergency margins and hard braking are rarer, the ego lower quartile is nearly unchanged, steering outcomes are unsupported, and no human preference, discomfort or task-interference outcome is measured. “Comfortable interactions stop being comfortable” is therefore an interpretation, not a result. A counterpart slowing more can represent burden, successful coordination, appropriate deference, or a normal response to priority; the current design cannot choose among these. At NMI level, a newly named “compression of ordinary interaction quality” cannot substitute for a validated outcome construct. The authors should either add independent human-centred outcomes (experienced comfort, predictability, acceptability, perceived pressure, intervention demand) and demonstrate incremental validity, or restrict the conclusion to the exact kinematic associations and avoid language implying diminished interaction quality.

8. **[Introduction; Sections 2.3–2.6; Methods 4.6 and planner interface] — The novelty and practical value are not demonstrated against appropriate baselines.** The technical ingredients—IPV/SVO-style inverse planning, human-reference distributions, conformal calibration and reject options—are established ideas, several from the authors' prior work. The potentially important contribution is their integration into an online monitor, but the manuscript does not compare that monitor with simpler alternatives: conditional trajectory likelihood, behaviour-cloning residual, yielding/priority heuristics, courtesy cost, acceleration/jerk, TTC-based state models, or existing anomaly/surprise monitors. It is therefore unknown whether IPV provides any additional information for flagging, calibration or the reported consequence signature. End-to-end real-time performance on vehicle hardware, latency, resource use, warning persistence, episode false-alarm rate and detection delay are explicitly unevaluated. A serious comparative benchmark at matched abstention/coverage, plus runtime and sequential-warning evaluation, is necessary to support claims of a useful planner-agnostic runtime monitor. Otherwise the contribution is a thoughtful evaluation framework rather than a validated machine-intelligence system.

## 3. Minor issues

1. **[Methods 4.3]** Specify the conditional quantile model class, optimisation, hyperparameter selection, feature encoding and all frozen feature definitions in the manuscript or an accessible supplement.
2. **[Methods 4.2–4.4]** Provide a complete flow diagram from 38,228 interactions to 26,828 ledger cases, 4,497,368 anchor rows, 2,442,625 human-reference rows and each split, with every exclusion and its reason.
3. **[Methods 4.5]** Define the duration of the ego post-verdict margin window; it is not stated as clearly as the counterpart's fixed three-second window.
4. **[Methods 4.5]** Report the number of placebo permutations and the exact construction underlying \(p=0.0199\).
5. **[Fig. 2a]** Formally define the plotted “change in how sharply the reading is identified,” including its scale and reference quantity.
6. **[Fig. 4c]** Add uncertainty for the 20.9% variance-explained estimate and show performance by source; the pooled point estimate hides the source failures shown in Fig. 3c.
7. **[Fig. 5]** Use independent alert episodes or runs as the displayed sample unit. The current moment counts invite pseudoreplication despite the run bootstrap described in the legend.
8. **[Fig. 6]** Add confidence intervals to panels a and c and identify which comparisons were pre-specified as inferential rather than descriptive.
9. **[Fig. ED1]** Correct “changes width with with the situation.”
10. **[Methods 4.4]** Explain the discrepancy between natural-corpus solver failures and “no engineering failures” on benchmark candidate moments, including how solver failures affect denominators.
11. **[Sections 2.2–2.3]** Quantify the event-level partner correlation and the uncertainty of every claimed ablation, not only the counterpart-IPV interval-score test.
12. **[Throughout]** Replace “competent humans” with a population-specific description unless competence was independently assessed; the reference comprises selected recorded drivers, not a validated competence standard.
13. **[Data and Code Availability]** A reproducible archive containing frozen splits, preprocessing, exclusions, verdict ledgers and analysis code should be available during evaluation rather than promised only upon publication.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What independent evidence shows that IPV corresponds to perceived courtesy, assertiveness, acceptability or social preference rather than merely to a fitted trajectory trade-off?
2. Can a simple model using only relative speed, acceleration, priority, distance and TTC reproduce the same flags and Fig. 5 consequence associations? What incremental value does IPV provide at matched coverage and abstention?
3. How sensitive are verdicts and sign labels to the candidate utility, seven-point grid, \(\sigma=0.1\) m, one-second window and map-centreline reference? What proportion of individual frame verdicts changes under reasonable alternatives?
4. How often do verdicts switch among within-range, competitive deviation, over-yielding and abstention within one interaction? What are alert-episode duration and episode-level false-alarm rates?
5. Does the Fig. 5 result remain after defining a single alert onset per episode, inserting a temporal guard gap, matching full pre-onset trajectories and analysing change from a pre-alert baseline?
6. Why is the greater counterpart deceleration interpreted as a post-flag consequence when Fig. ED2 shows deceleration beginning before the selected flagged stretch?
7. What are the estimates under crossed resampling or hierarchical modelling by scenario template, automated system/run and, for the human arm, participant?
8. How many candidate, readable, supported and flagged moments occur for each scenario, automated system and human participant? Is the 2.1-fold ratio preserved after common-support standardisation?
9. Why are there 267 automated-system runs rather than the 285 implied by 19 systems × 15 scenarios, and were missing runs related to system behaviour or scenario difficulty?
10. What were the human-study ethics approval, consent and safety procedures, participant characteristics, scenario order and counterbalancing, practice protocol and missing-data rules?
11. What two-sided criterion would count as successful target-domain calibration? Is 4.7% outside a nominal 90% interval regarded as acceptable, and if so, on what pre-specified basis?
12. What is the monitor's latency and compute demand on target vehicle hardware, and how do map, perception and counterpart-association errors affect abstention and false alerts?
13. Were any consequence endpoints, thresholds, display criteria or analysis choices examined before the “frozen” battery was set? Where is the time-stamped analysis plan?
14. Are the 19 systems statistically and algorithmically independent, or do some share teams, planners, codebases or repeated submissions?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Validate the construct independently.** Add blinded human-centred and controlled behavioural validation showing that IPV and range violations measure socially meaningful preference/compliance beyond kinematics; otherwise retitle and narrow all claims to conditional trajectory atypicality.
2. **Rebuild the consequence analysis around independent alert episodes.** Use onset times, a non-overlapping future window, temporal guard gap, pre-trend/change analysis, strong state matching and negative controls; remove causal or quality language not supported by that design.
3. **Reanalyse the benchmark and human arm with the correct hierarchy.** Model or resample scenario, system/run and participant effects; standardise common support; quantify uncertainty for Fig. 6; and control the endpoint family.
4. **Demonstrate selective calibration under shift.** Report coverage, width, abstention and alarm rate jointly across sources, scenarios, roles, risk groups, systems and human participants, with pre-specified two-sided target-domain criteria.
5. **Establish measurement robustness and added value.** Propagate estimator uncertainty, perform specification sensitivity and known-truth recovery tests, and compare against strong simple and existing social/anomaly baselines at matched acceptance coverage.
6. **Complete the human-study and reproducibility record.** Supply ethics and protocol details, participant/run accounting, frozen analysis plan, data lineage, model specification and reviewable code/data artifacts.
7. **Evaluate the claimed runtime interface.** Report end-to-end latency and failures on relevant hardware, map/perception perturbations, warning persistence, episode false alarms and detection delay before describing the system as deployment-ready or planner-agnostic.

## 6. Acceptance probability

- **As submitted:** 5%.
- **Assuming a competent major revision that includes the independent validation and reanalysis above:** 35%.

The second probability remains below even because the most important remedies require new evidence, not only clearer exposition.

## 7. Recommendation

**Reject.** The manuscript contains a potentially valuable monitoring framework and unusually transparent boundary statements, but its central NMI-level claim currently rests on an unvalidated social construct, a temporally endogenous consequence analysis and insufficiently supported transfer and human–machine comparisons. A substantially new version could become competitive if it establishes independent social validity and survives episode-level, hierarchical and distribution-shift-aware evaluation.
