# Reviewer B

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes a pointwise monitor that estimates an interaction preference value (IPV) from a one-second trajectory prefix, abstains when the estimate or human support is inadequate, and compares accepted estimates with a situation-conditioned human reference interval. It reports near-nominal pooled moment-level coverage in held-out natural-driving data, marked loss of coverage under some leave-one-source-out shifts, and associations between assertive-side flags and compressed—but not more emergency-prone—interactions in a matched-scenario vehicle benchmark. A matched human arm is used to argue that the reference transfers and that automated systems are flagged more often.

The central idea—explicit abstention plus a conditional human reference—is potentially useful. However, the work presently establishes an empirical, pointwise atypicality detector, not a validated runtime-verification or runtime-assurance mechanism. The conformal claim does not address clustered sequential data, no warning policy or trajectory-level error guarantee is evaluated, end-to-end real-time feasibility is untested, IPV lacks independent construct validation, and the comparison with conventional safety monitoring is not made against any actual safety monitor. The human-arm transfer and automated-versus-human claims also lack the crossed, cluster-aware inference needed for their prominence. These are central rather than presentational deficiencies.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Sections 2.3, 4.4 and Algorithm 2] — The reported conformal calibration does not establish the sequential or cluster-level validity required of a runtime monitor.** The conformal radius is computed by pooling 455,723 accepted calibration moments, while moments within scenes and interactions are explicitly dependent. Splitting whole scenes prevents train/test leakage, but it does not by itself make the pooled moment-level nonconformity scores exchangeable at the unit at which a new deployment episode arrives. Variable numbers of accepted moments per scene additionally induce size weighting. The authors correctly call the result “empirical” and “marginal over accepted moments,” but elsewhere repeatedly use “calibrated runtime monitor” in a stronger sense. A nominal 10% pointwise outside rate can yield a very high probability of at least one flag in a sufficiently long interaction, even under the human reference distribution. This is exactly the quantity relevant to warnings and interventions. At NMI level, the statistical contract of the monitor must match the operational object. The remedy is to calibrate and test at an independent unit such as scene, interaction or run; use an appropriate clustered/hierarchical, block or trajectory-level conformal construction; and report trajectory-wise false-alarm probability, time-uniform coverage or another explicitly defined sequential guarantee. If only pooled pointwise empirical coverage is retained, all claims of conformal runtime validity should be narrowed accordingly.

2. **[Methods 4.4, Algorithm 2 and Planner interface] — There is no evaluated temporal verdict or warning semantics.** Every result is per moment, while persistence is deferred to an optional, unevaluated deployment interface. Consequently, the manuscript provides no definition or evidence for alarm onset, duration, chattering, recovery, minimum dwell time, detection delay, repeated testing, or episode-level false alarms. “Abstain,” “within norm” and the two deviation labels form a pointwise classifier, not yet a runtime-verification specification over a signal. This omission is particularly consequential because Fig. 2b shows that readable estimates move more, not less, and Fig. 2c shows that plausible temporal summaries change the answer. A competent revision should freeze a temporal contract—for example a precisely specified persistence/state-machine or temporal-logic rule—before evaluation, and report run-level operating characteristics on full streams. The rule must specify how gaps and each abstention reason affect timers, whether evidence accumulates across gaps, how conflicting upper- and lower-side deviations are handled, and how the monitor resets. Without this layer, the paper should avoid implying that the reported per-frame results constitute a deployable warning monitor.

3. **[Methods 4.1, 4.2, 4.4; Algorithm 2] — “Online-computable” is not evidence of real-time executability or causal deployability.** The paper explicitly states that end-to-end real-time operation on vehicle hardware is not evaluated. It gives no wall-clock latency, worst-case or high-quantile execution time, deadline, memory/compute demand, scheduling assumption, sensor-to-verdict delay, timestamp synchronization error, or behavior under dropped/delayed inputs. The absence of solver failures on 67,861 benchmark candidate moments addresses numerical completion, not whether the verdict arrives before it can be acted upon. A runtime-assurance claim requires at minimum a causal streaming replay using only data available at each timestamp, measured end-to-end latency on a specified platform, deadline-miss statistics, and an explicit response to stale or missing estimates. Robustness to perception, map, localization and counterpart-association uncertainty should be included in that replay. Otherwise, the contribution should be labelled an offline replay evaluation of an online-computable statistic.

4. **[Sections 2.1, 4.1 and Figs. 1–2] — The IPV has not been independently validated as the claimed social-preference construct.** The controls in Fig. 2a show that the candidate likelihoods concentrate more in aligned interactions; they do not show that the resulting weighted grid value recovers how an agent trades its own progress against the group’s. Agreement with an offline estimator using the same inverse-planning model is also not construct validation. The same model generates the human reference and the monitored value, so calibration of its output distribution can succeed even if the model is systematically wrong about preference. This is a central circularity: the paper robustly detects deviation in a model-derived coordinate, but interprets that coordinate as social preference and titles the result “social compliance.” The authors need independent validation against controlled behavior with manipulated incentives or known interaction trade-offs, convergent human judgments, or another external criterion not generated by the same estimator. They should quantify identifiability rather than equate a maximum candidate weight above 0.20 with a meaningful reading. If such validation is unavailable, the title and claims should be narrowed to context-conditioned atypicality of an inverse-planning score.

5. **[Methods 4.1–4.4] — Estimation and verdict uncertainty are not propagated through the monitor, and robustness to model misspecification is untested.** A seven-point candidate grid, a fixed one-second window, a fixed Gaussian position error scale of 0.1 m and a maximum-weight gate of 0.20 determine both the estimated IPV and whether a reading exists. The manuscript gives no sensitivity analysis for these choices and no evaluation under realistic localization noise, map-reference error, trajectory smoothing, sampling-rate changes, missing frames, alternative route hypotheses, identity switches or candidate-trajectory model mismatch. A point estimate is then compared with a quantile boundary as though the estimate were observed without error. This can create unstable flags near either boundary and can make the learned reference absorb estimator-specific biases rather than resolve them. The required remedy is a preregistered robustness battery varying the grid, window, likelihood scale and gate; perturbation tests matched to realistic sensing and map errors; reporting of boundary-crossing stability; and either propagation of estimator uncertainty into the reference-membership decision or a demonstrated conservative margin. Selective performance should be reported as risk-versus-coverage curves, not only at one frozen threshold.

6. **[Sections 2.3, 2.5, 4.4, 4.6 and Fig. 6] — The evidence does not establish transfer of the reference across sources, countries, apparatus and tasks.** The manuscript itself reports 90% leave-one-source-out coverage of only 0.743 for Waymo and 0.750 for Lyft, alongside 0.990 for nuPlan and 0.900 for Argoverse-2. These results demonstrate substantial source sensitivity. In the matched human arm, a 4.7% flag fraction among judgeable moments is described as successful transfer because it does not exceed the 9.72% native outside rate. A low selective flag rate, however, is not evidence of calibration: it can arise from conservative intervals, changed case mix, or selective abstention. The manuscript does not provide the human arm’s complete candidate → active/readable → supported/judgeable accounting, reason-specific abstention rates, or coverage-like results by scenario and driver. The support gate can also hide, rather than solve, shift. The authors should state a pre-specified transfer estimand and equivalence/non-inferiority margin, report uncertainty with driver and scenario clustering, show the full verdict-plus-abstention profile, and evaluate conditional performance across the relevant situation strata. The current evidence supports bounded empirical behavior on selected accepted moments, not the broad transfer claim.

7. **[Section 2.5, Methods 4.6 and Fig. 6] — The “2.1 times as often” automated-versus-human result is not supported by an appropriate crossed analysis.** The experimental units are 19 automated systems and 20 human drivers crossed with 15 scenarios, yet the headline comparison is made using 14,099 versus 15,102 judgeable moments. Those moments are highly repeated within system/driver and scenario, and the number of accepted moments is itself behavior-dependent. “15/15 scenarios above parity” is descriptive, not an uncertainty analysis. No confidence interval is shown for the 2.1 ratio, panels 6a and 6c explicitly omit uncertainty, and no model accounts for driver/system, scenario and run effects. This makes the most prominent comparative conclusion vulnerable to pseudoreplication and unequal judgeability. The revision should use a pre-specified crossed or hierarchical analysis at the run level, report per-driver and per-system distributions, paired scenario effects with confidence intervals, and sensitivity to denominator choice (candidate, active, readable, supported and judgeable moments). Flag rate and abstention rate should be analysed jointly. The claim should remain population- and benchmark-specific, not a ranking of “automated systems” against humans in general.

8. **[Sections 2.4, 4.5 and Fig. 5] — The consequence evidence remains exploratory and does not yet validate the semantic meaning of a flag.** Resampling scenario runs is preferable to treating moments as independent, but the analysis still compares many overlapping post-verdict windows and a battery of nested thresholds and endpoints. Intervals are explicitly per endpoint, with no simultaneous error control. The timing-placebo result (200 reallocations, empirical p = 0.0199) is positive, whereas the case-level label permutation is not (p = 0.1493), and the open-ended 90% contract-window interval crosses zero. These mixed results are not integrated into the strength of the main claim. Moreover, the same latent interaction state can drive both the flag and the subsequent tightening; temporal precedence of the one-second estimation window does not remove this confounding. The paper appropriately disclaims causality, but phrases such as “the flag means the same thing” overstate what is shown. The authors should nominate one primary consequence estimand, account for endpoint multiplicity, use non-overlapping or event-level summaries, provide within-run/scenario matched controls, and report effect heterogeneity. The conclusion should remain an association between flags and subsequent trajectories unless a design capable of identifying intervention effects is supplied.

9. **[Section 2.6 and Discussion] — The claim that social monitoring registers something conventional safety monitoring cannot see is not tested.** The comparison is only against realized TTC and braking thresholds. Runtime safety monitors based on RSS-style safe-distance rules, reachable-set intersection, barrier-function robustness, predicted collision risk, or temporal-logic robustness need not wait for a binary emergency threshold; several would react to shrinking margins before a violation. The observation that flagged moments have lower emergency-tail frequency does not imply that “a monitor built on those thresholds would report nothing” in general, nor does it establish incremental information beyond a continuous safety robustness signal. This distinction is central to the paper’s positioning. The authors should run representative causal safety monitors on the same timestamped streams and quantify overlap, disagreement, lead time and incremental predictive/explanatory value after conditioning on their robustness scores. Alternatively, Section 2.6 must be narrowed to the exact post-verdict TTC and braking thresholds evaluated here, without general claims about formal safety verification.

10. **[Methods 4.1–4.4 and Algorithms 1–2] — The monitor is not specified sufficiently for independent reproduction or assurance review.** The cost terms and candidate-trajectory generator are deferred to future code release; the conditional quantile model family, hyperparameters, optimization and feature definitions are not stated; the precise online risk proxies are unnamed; the map/role and out-of-distribution gates are not operationally defined; most gate thresholds and their selection objectives are absent; and Algorithm 2 collapses failures to the first reason code, preventing the reader from seeing co-occurring failure causes. These are not implementation trivia: they determine the property being monitored, the support set and the accepted population on which calibration is claimed. The Methods should provide a complete executable specification or sufficiently detailed pseudocode and parameter tables, including gate ordering and all constants. An audit should report every gate both exclusively and non-exclusively, so the reason-code order does not obscure failure modes.

11. **[Methods 4.6] — The human-participant study lacks essential methodological and ethical information.** The text states only that 20 licensed drivers completed 15 scenarios. It does not report ethics approval, informed consent, recruitment, demographics, driving experience, inclusion/exclusion criteria, compensation, scenario order and counterbalancing, familiarization, repeated-exposure effects, safety supervision, intervention procedures, or missing-run handling. These factors can materially alter both social behavior and flag rate in a staged closed-course task. They are also required to assess whether the “same scenarios” comparison is fair and whether the human arm can support a transfer claim. A full human-participant protocol, ethics statement, participant flow, power rationale and run-level accounting are required.

12. **[Planner interface and Discussion] — The paper does not provide a safety case for using the output in a runtime-assurance architecture.** The planner mappings—soft cost, warning, fallback under sustained deviation, and degraded mode on abstention—are suggestions, not evaluated contracts. There is no hazard analysis of false competitive-deviation flags, false within-norm outputs, prolonged abstention, or feedback loops created when the planner reacts to an estimator trained on unreactive human records. There is also no arbitration specification proving that the social layer cannot delay or conflict with the collision-safety layer. Merely stating subordination does not establish non-interference. If planner-facing use remains part of the contribution, the authors should define the assurance boundary, assumptions/guarantees, monitor health state, fail-silent behavior, arbitration priority and a minimal closed-loop evaluation. Otherwise, these action mappings should be removed from the claimed contribution and presented only as future work.

## 3. Minor issues

1. The title says “socially compliant,” whereas the manuscript repeatedly and correctly states that atypicality is neither inappropriateness nor harm. The title should reflect the narrower object actually validated.
2. Report confidence intervals for every headline estimate, including the middle-risk value in Section 2.2 and the 2.1 automated/human rate ratio.
3. In Fig. 5 and the surrounding text, make the transition from 519 assertive-side judgeable moments to 472 moments with a defined ego margin visually explicit; the current denominators require careful cross-reading.
4. State whether the 175 non-scripted runs and the 120 runs containing assertive flags were fixed before any endpoint analysis, and give their distribution across the 19 systems and 15 scenarios.
5. “Supported threshold” in Fig. 5 is easily confused with “human support” in the monitor. Use distinct terminology.
6. Clarify whether the reported bootstrap intervals are percentile, basic or studentized intervals and whether small-cluster corrections were used.
7. The 80%, 90% and 95% interval results reuse the same accepted moments; state whether quantile rearrangement preserves nesting after conformal widening in every evaluated state.
8. Report the distribution, not only the mean, of conditional interval width by source and key state groups. A mean width of 1.87 rad over a 2.36-rad candidate span remains very broad.
9. “No engineering failures” should be replaced by “no recorded solver or pipeline failures in this replay,” because timing, sensing and integration failures were not tested.
10. Make the terminology for the two outside states consistent across the text, algorithms and figures: “competitive deviation,” “assertive-side atypical,” “below range,” “over-yielding,” and “above range” currently alternate.
11. Explain how ties exactly on a conformal interval boundary are classified and how floating-point tolerances are handled.
12. The Data and Code Availability statements promise release only upon publication. Review of the central numerical claims would be materially strengthened by anonymous access to the derived streams, split ledger and frozen analysis code.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What is the exact statistical guarantee you claim for a new interaction trajectory, as distinct from a uniformly sampled accepted moment from the pooled test rows?
2. How many candidate, active, readable, supported and judgeable moments occurred in the human arm, broken down by driver and scenario, and what were all exclusive and non-exclusive abstention reasons?
3. What are the episode-level probabilities of at least one assertive-side flag, at least one over-yielding flag and any flag for held-out human interactions at each nominal level?
4. How would Algorithm 2 treat alternating deviations and abstentions under the proposed persistence interface, and what are the resulting detection delays and warning durations?
5. What platform and causal data pipeline were used for replay, and what are median, 95th-, 99th- and worst-case sensor-to-verdict latencies relative to the intended update deadline?
6. How were the 0.1 m likelihood scale, one-second window, seven-point grid and 0.20 maximum-weight threshold selected? Were any downstream results inspected before these choices were frozen?
7. Which exact variables constitute the online risk proxies, “support/readability state” and out-of-distribution test, and can each be computed without future information under sensor latency?
8. What independent evidence shows that the IPV estimates preference rather than merely fitting trajectory geometry under the chosen utility family?
9. What are the confidence interval and hierarchical model specification for the 2.1 automated/human flag-rate ratio? Does it persist using runs rather than moments as the unit?
10. Does the automated/human comparison persist when the denominator is all candidate moments and when abstention is modelled as a separate outcome?
11. Why is a 4.7% matched-human outside rate against a nominal 10% rate interpreted as successful calibration rather than overcoverage or selection? What equivalence margin was specified?
12. How do representative RSS, reachability-based, predicted-collision-risk or continuous safety-robustness monitors behave at the same flagged timestamps?
13. How do the Fig. 5 effects change under one non-overlapping summary per run or flagged episode, with scenario and system effects included?
14. What ethical approval, consent and safety protocol governed the 20-driver study, and how were order, learning and repeated exposure to the 15 staged conflicts handled?
15. What assumption guarantees that a planner response to the social flag cannot invalidate estimator calibration or interfere with the higher-priority collision-safety controller?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Define and validate the actual sequential monitor.** Freeze temporal verdict semantics and report trajectory/run-level false alarms, detection delay, duration, chattering and abstention behavior.
2. **Repair the calibration claim at the deployment unit.** Use scene/interaction/run-aware calibration and inference, or explicitly limit the paper to pooled pointwise empirical coverage without a sequential conformal guarantee.
3. **Reanalyse the human arm and automated/human comparison.** Provide the full selection/abstention ledger, uncertainty, a crossed driver-or-system × scenario model, run-level sensitivity analyses and a pre-specified transfer criterion.
4. **Validate or narrow the IPV interpretation.** Supply independent construct validation and estimator-robustness tests; otherwise retitle and reframe the work as monitoring a model-derived interaction-atypicality score.
5. **Test the claimed distinction from safety monitoring.** Compare against actual causal safety-monitor outputs and continuous robustness measures on the same streams, or sharply narrow Section 2.6.
6. **Demonstrate causal streaming feasibility.** Report end-to-end runtime, deadlines, failures and perturbation robustness on a specified platform using only timestamp-available inputs.
7. **Make the monitor reproducible.** Fully specify the estimator, reference model, feature set, every gate, all thresholds, split logic and gate-level accounting; provide anonymous code and derived data for review.
8. **Strengthen the consequence analysis.** Predeclare a primary endpoint, address endpoint multiplicity and overlapping windows, use event/run-level matched analyses, and align the prose with the non-causal evidence.
9. **Complete the human-participant Methods and deployment assurance boundary.** Add ethics and protocol details, and either provide a concrete non-interference/assurance argument for planner use or remove planner-facing claims from the present contribution.

## 6. Acceptance probability

- **As submitted:** 8%.
- **Assuming a competent major revision that completes the analyses and validation above:** 45%.

The second probability remains below even because several remedies require new sequential, runtime, human-study and comparator evidence rather than textual clarification alone.

## 7. Recommendation

**Major revision.**

The empirical framework is promising enough to justify revision, but the paper’s central language currently outruns its evidence. A publishable version must clearly choose between two positions: a carefully bounded, pointwise empirical monitor of conditional IPV atypicality, or a genuine runtime-verification/assurance contribution with sequential semantics, cluster-valid calibration, causal real-time execution, comparator monitors and an explicit assurance boundary.
