                                                Abstract

      Autonomous vehicles are engineered and assessed for collision safety, yet a crash-free

      manoeuvre can still be socially atypical. We recast social compliance as an online

      monitoring question: conditional social atypicalitywhether the vehicle's current

      interaction behaviour lies within the human range for its situation. The interaction

      preference valuehow an agent trades its own progress against the group'sis in-

      terpretable only while an interaction is active and the estimate reliable; otherwise

      the monitor abstains. Human social behaviour is context-dependent; conditioning

      the reference range on the current observable situation gives a sharper, calibrated,

      auditable runtime monitor. On a matched-scenario real-vehicle benchmark, mo-

      ments agged on the assertive side are followed by tighter interactions on both

      sides, with no rise in emergency rates at any supported threshold. Auditing the ref-

      erence with its dening populationhuman drivers in the same scenariosshows

      no alarm ination, the same consequence signature, and automated systems agged

      about twice as often. Atypicality is not yet inappropriateness: whether a deviation

      is harmful is the next validation layer, which the monitor makes testable.

1      Introduction
Autonomous vehicles (AVs) must share the road with human drivers, cyclists and pedes-


trians whose behaviour is shaped not only by physical constraints but also by implicit


social norms [13]. A vehicle can be   collision-safe yet not interaction-benign : a manoeu-
vre that is collision-free in isolation may still be aggressive, hesitant or norm-violating in


its interaction context, eroding the predictability on which mixed humanmachine trac


depends [46].   Machine behaviour in human collectives is now an object of empirical


study in its own right [7]: controlled experiments show that machine agents embedded in


human groups reshape coordination and cooperation [810], and can suppress benecial


social norms such as reciprocity, with collective consequences [11]. Conventional safety


verication asks will this trajectory collide; a socially aware AV also faces a dierent


question: is this way of interacting still understandable and acceptable to the humans


around it?


    Two established families of methods address adjacent but distinct questions. Oine


behavioural evaluation asks whether an AV, on average, drove like a human, typically by


reporting a single aggregate sociality score over a dataset [1215], and no test-mileage


budget can in any case certify rare interactive behaviour by aggregate demonstration [16,


17].   Formal safety verication asks whether a trajectory will collide or violate a hard


constraint, and can guarantee a formally specied notion of legal safety  within the


assumptions of its model  as an online layer around a planner [1821], an assurance now


being codied in standards [22, 23].   Neither answers the moment-to-moment question:


given the current interaction state, is this behaviour within the range that human drivers


exhibit in the same situation? That question is local in time, conditional on context, and


graded rather than binary.


    We therefore make autonomous-driving social deviation       visible online, and separate
deviations that are merely uncommon from those a human would disprefer or that burden


the interaction. We do not claim that every departure from human behaviour is wrong.


Instead we monitor deviation from a human interaction range, we separate the moments


at which a social judgement can be made from those at which it cannot, and we lay out
Online Social-Compliance Monitoring for AVs                                               3



the validation path along which atypicality may acquire normative meaning:




         human reference range    → conditional social atypicality →

                human-dispreferred behaviour   → interaction-harmful behaviour.


The monitored quantity is the interaction preference value (IPV) [24, 25], a trajectory-


derived measure of how an agent balances its own progress against the group's. It belongs


to the family of preference constructs recovered from observed behavioursocial value


orientation in games and in driving [26, 27], and reward inference from trajectories [28,


29]estimated here online, within a single interaction.


   Three distinctions bound the claims. First,    socially atypical is not socially inappro-
priate : lying outside the human range is a statement about typicality, not a normative
verdict, and acquires normative force only when it is shown to track human disprefer-


ence or interaction harm.    Second,   collision-safe is not interaction-benign. Third, not
readable is not neutral : before an interaction is behaviourally informative or after it has
resolved, the IPV may be weakly identiable or not applicable, and this must not be read


as compliant.    A responsible social monitor must know when      not to judge; abstention
is an explicit boundary of responsible judgement, not a failure mode, in the tradition


of prediction with a reject option [3032].    The human range is, moreover, a    reference
range in the sense familiar from clinical medicine and normative modelling [3335], not
a demand to imitate one human trajectory.


   This framing diers from three existing paradigms. Unlike oine AV evaluation [12,


13], it is online, per-timestep and context-conditioned.    Unlike runtime verication of


formal specications [3639], the monitored property is an empirical normative distribu-


tion estimated from human data. Unlike socially compliant planning [27, 4043], it does


not encode sociality into a planner's objective but provides a pluggable, planner-agnostic


monitor that any planner can be checked against. Closest to our aim, online monitors


exist for codied legal rules [44]; unusual driving can be agged as unexplained deviation


from learned trac behaviour [45] or as violated expectation [46, 47]; and AV conduct is


benchmarked oine against reference models of a competent human driver [48, 49]. None
Online Social-Compliance Monitoring for AVs                                                4



of these reads a social preference, refers it to a calibrated situation-conditioned human


range, or separates the moments at which a social judgement is meaningful from those


at which it is not.


    This work reframes social compliance as the online monitoring of conditional social


atypicality. The reframing rests on a readability boundary: a social judgement is mean-


ingful only while an interaction is behaviourally informative and the reading is identiable,


and elsewhere the monitor abstains rather than passing the vehicle. Human social be-


haviour is context-dependent, and although interacting partners' preferences co-vary, the


reference range needed online turns out to require only the current observable situation,


so the monitor never has to read a hidden intention. Built on that context-conditioned


range, it is calibrated and auditable at runtime. We then ask what an atypicality verdict


corresponds to in the interaction that follows, on a matched-scenario real-vehicle bench-


mark, and report its status honestly: the evidence establishes a compression of ordinary


interaction quality on both sides, while   bounding rather than conrming harm prediction.
Finally we close the loop on the reference itself. The population that dened it drives


the same scenarios under the same monitor, which shows that the reference transfers


across country, apparatus and task without inating its alarms, that the agged-moment


signature is a property of the interaction rather than of machines, and that under this


common instrument the automated systems are agged about twice as often as matched


human drivers.

2.2 Human social behaviour is context-dependent
We next characterise human behaviour at the level of a whole interaction, summarising


each agent by the mean IPV over its valid interaction segment.                                                                                  Complete-interaction


behaviour does not reduce to a single global sociality score. We analysed 38,228 interac-
Online Social-Compliance Monitoring for AVs                                                 8



tion cases from four naturalistic sources within the InterHub corpus [50] (Waymo 23,218;


nuPlan 7,499; Lyft 5,105; Argoverse-2 2,406; 3,695,981 trajectory frames).        The social


meaning of right-of-way is organised by the interaction state (Fig. 3): the dierence in


IPV between the priority and non-priority agent reverses sign with collision risk, from


+0.058 (95% CI [+0.050, +0.067], n = 9,799) under high risk (post-encroachment time
≤ 1.0 s), through +0.001 (n = 22,833) at intermediate risk, to −0.034 ([−0.045, −0.023],
n = 4,863) under low risk (> 2.0 s). The role shifts are small against the width of the
human range  a few per cent of it; what conditioning contributes operationally is the


range sharpening of Fig. 4  but their direction reverses with risk, which no scalar


score can represent.     A coarse geometric prior is prosocial across all four sources, and


the direction of the risk-gated eect is preserved under dropping the largest source and


under alternative risk and geometry binnings. The two interacting agents' preferences,


moreover, are   correlated at this event level: social behaviour is a relational quantity, or-
ganised by role, geometry and risk, not a xed disposition of an individual. Whatever


the human reference is, it must therefore be conditioned on the interaction state; a single


scalar sociality score cannot serve as that reference. We report this as a state-dependent


regularity of the present corpora, not as a transferable cross-dataset law.

2.4 From atypicality to interaction consequences
We ask what an atypicality verdict corresponds to in the interaction that follows, when


the same situations are driven by dierent automated systems.         We pursue this on a


matched-scenario real-vehicle benchmark [53], in which identical scenarios are replayed


against many independent driving systems.


   The monitor is, rst, able to speak.      The benchmark replays 15 scenario templates


against 19 independent driving systems (267 scenario runs). Across its 67,861 candidate


moments the monitor returns a verdict on 14,099 (20.8%), with no engineering failures


in this replay  no solver or pipeline failure on any candidate moment  and in 231


of the 267 scenario runs (86.5%) it speaks at least once. At the moment level, 55.3% of


candidate moments carry discriminative information between the candidates, and 20.8%


additionally lie within human support; at the run level, none of the 36 runs in which


the monitor never speaks is silenced by readability alone  in every such run there are


readable moments, and what is missing there is comparable human behaviour for the


situation.   Abstention here is a statement about the reference rather than about the


vehicle, and it is auditable as such.


   Where the monitor does speak, atypicality on the assertive sidea current prefer-


ence that weights the counterpart's cost less than humans do in the same situationis

2.6 Social monitoring and collision safety register dierent things
Finally, what does a social ag register that a conventional safety check does not? The


previous section sharpens the answer. The compression it documents is precisely what


a threshold-based check cannot see: at no emergency threshold are the agged moments


more frequent than the typical onesat the thresholds whose intervals exclude a zero


dierence (Section 2.4) they are one-third to one-half as frequentso a monitor built
Online Social-Compliance Monitoring for AVs                                              17



on those thresholds would report nothing, while the interaction has nonetheless become


measurably tighter for both vehicles. Social atypicality and conventional safety therefore


register dierent things.




3    Discussion
This work does not turn social compliance into a single score. It turns it into an auditable


monitoring question: is the AV's current interaction behaviour within the human range


for this situation, and if not, is that deviation meaningful enough to matter?         Four


results support the question. The IPV is an online, interaction-conditioned measurement


that is interpretable only when an interaction is active and the estimate reliable, so a


responsible monitor abstains rather than assume neutrality. Human social behaviour is


context-dependent, organised by risk, role and geometry, so the reference cannot be a


scalar. A reference range conditioned on the current observable situation is sharp and


near-nominally calibrated, giving an auditable monitor of conditional social atypicality.


And the reference is not an article of faith: carried to a new country, apparatus and task,


it ags its own dening population no more often than at home, and what it ags carries


the same consequence signature whether the driver is a human or a machine.


    The most informative nding is a tension between two levels. At the level of a whole


interaction the partners' preferences are correlated; yet online, conditioning the reference


range on the counterpart's inferred preferenceor on the ego agent's own historyadds


no measurable value beyond the current situation. The relational structure that is visible


across interactions is already carried by the observable situation, so a runtime social


monitor does not need to read the other agent's hidden intention. This both simplies


deployment and claries what social compliance online    is : a question about behaviour in
a situation, not about inferred minds.


    The claims are deliberately bounded.     Socially atypical is not socially inappropri-


ate; collision-safe is not interaction-benign; and not readable is not neutral. Atypicality


becomes normatively loaded only along the validation route, and we report its honest
Online Social-Compliance Monitoring for AVs                                                   18



status: the real-vehicle route establishes a compression of ordinary interaction quality on


both sides, while currently bounding rather than conrming a harm relationship, because


identifying interaction-failure events accurately is itself an open measurement problem.


Rather than a weakness, this denes a concrete next problemreliable interaction-failure


segmentation and adequately powered outcome datacreated by, not hidden by, the


present monitor. The evidence in this paper covers pairwise vehiclevehicle interactions


at mapped conict points; interactions with cyclists and pedestrians, and multi-agent


scenes, are outside the present reference and are the natural next extension of the same


construction. Beyond driving, the framework oers a template for monitoring whether


autonomous agents behave within human normative ranges online, under uncertainty


and distribution shift, while being explicit about when a social judgement should be


withheld [7].

4.2 Interaction opportunity, readability and episode summary
An interaction-opportunity indicator oij (t) marks when a behaviourally meaningful in-

downstream; ga = 0 states are labelled (no active interaction, insucient discriminative

4.3 Context-conditioned human reference range
The reference range is the conditional distribution of the human current IPV given the


current observable situation zt (geometry, role, interaction progress, relative kinematics,


online-computable risk proxies, and support/readability state), estimated by conditional


quantile regression (histogram-based gradient-boosted quantile models, one per quantile


level, with frozen hyperparameters: learning rate 0.06, 72 boosting iterations, maximum


31 leaf nodes, minimum 80 samples per leaf, L2 regularisation 0.01, 255 bins, no early
stopping) and evaluated only on readable, active windows. The reference pool contains


humanhuman vehicle pairs only (2,442,625 anchor rows): any interaction in which either


agent is an automated data-collection vehicle is excluded from the reference, so machine


behaviour cannot contaminate the human range. The corpora are eet-collected in spe-


cic cities in the United States and Singapore; the reference is a sample of natural driving


there, not of all human driving. We compare a global reference, an oine oracle-risk ceil-


ing, and the context-conditioned reference; the context-conditioned reference supplies


essentially all of the achievable sharpening over the global reference (width reductions of


21%, 20% and 8% at the 80%, 90% and 95% levels, at coverage within 0.6 percentage
Online Social-Compliance Monitoring for AVs                                                 21



points of nominal; the global 95% range spans essentially the whole admissible scale).


Two further inputs are evaluated as ablations and add no measurable value: condition-


ing on the principal counterpart's current IPV, and conditioning on the ego agent's own


behavioural history (the self-anchor). Neither is interpreted as a mechanism; the counter-


part channel in particular is statistically indistinguishable from removing the IPV input


given the situation (paired 90% interval-score dierence −0.0002, case-clustered p = 0.86),


whichset against the event-level partner correlationis the tension highlighted in the


Results. Concretely, zt comprises four categorical descriptors (path-geometry category,


path relation, turn-pair label and priority role) and 22 numeric channels: elapsed interac-


tion time and history length; ego and counterpart velocity components and headings at


the anchor; relative position components, distance, velocity components, speed, closing


rate and heading dierence at the anchor; short-window means and a dispersion of relative


distance, relative speed and closing rate; and two online risk proxies  the closing-rate


time-to-collision at the anchor and an anticipated post-encroachment proxy computed


only from information available at time t.      Excluded from the reference are observed


post-encroachment time, realised passing order, post-hoc phase labels, the episode sum-


mary, the machine/human identity, and target-proximal concurrent ego actions; quantiles


are made non-crossing by rearrangement. The reference range conditions on the current


situation rather than on a specic temporal pattern of the interaction; temporal-history


features were examined and are not part of the primary conditioning.

4.5 Real-vehicle consequence protocol
We use a matched-scenario real-vehicle benchmark at the scenario level (no run-level,


seed-level or causal claims), separating an outcome universe from a replay/IPV universe


with frozen exclusions. The counterpart vehicles are driven by trac-simulation software


(TESS NG) and respond to what the ego vehicle does; the same counterpart control is


used in the human reference arm below, so the two arms face a counterpart that reacts to


them on the same terms. The staging is mixed-reality: the ego is a real vehicle driven at


a test site, and the simulated counterparts reach an automated system through its per-


ception interface and a human driver through a head-mounted display. No conrmatory


analysis proceeded until an outcome-blind crosswalk had reconciled canonical scenario


identiers, structural case identities and ocial outcome labels.   Realised-consequence


events are dened by a frozen automatic extractor.      Verdicts are issued per moment;


outcome windows begin at the agged moment, so the trajectory samples that produce a


verdict (the one-second window ending at that moment) precede every outcome sample.


The ego-side outcome is the minimum time-to-collision to the counterpart over the post-


verdict window, which runs from the verdict to the end of that run's evaluated window


(the counterpart-side battery instead uses the xed three-second window below); time-


to-collision is computed per frame as distance over closing rate, and frames in which the


pair is not closing do not enter the minimum; the counterpart-side outcomes are read


over a xed three-second window from the same moment, from the other vehicle's own


logged control record. Comparisons are made within the frozen situation cells, inference


resamples scenario runs (the unit of clustering throughout), and a placebo test that re-


assigns whole exposure trajectories across scenario runs distinguishes the real ag timing


from permuted timings (200 whole-trajectory reassignment draws; the comparison statis-


tic is the absolute case-clustered t; empirical p = 0.0199). Across all six levelwindow


combinations of the log(1 + TTC) magnitude battery the coecients agree in sign; the


xed three-second window is the pre-specied primary, and its case-clustered intervals


exclude zero at all three levels, while the open-ended contract-window interval crosses
