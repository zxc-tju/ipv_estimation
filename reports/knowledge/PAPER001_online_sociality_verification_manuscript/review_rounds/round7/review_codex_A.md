# Referee A report

## 1. Summary assessment

The paper proposes an abstaining, context-conditioned monitor of whether an automated vehicle's estimated interaction preference value (IPV) lies within a human reference range. It explicitly limits the target to “conditional social atypicality” and states that “Atypicality is not yet inappropriateness” (Abstract; Sections 2.3 and 3). The monitor is technically elaborate and the manuscript is unusually explicit about abstention and transfer boundaries. However, the central behavioural interpretation is not established. IPV remains a preference only under the authors' inverse-planning cost model; the presented controls show model discriminability, not construct validity. The hidden-intention claim exceeds its ablation, and the claimed “compression of ordinary interaction quality” (Section 2.4) can be explained by kinematic phase and a reactive simulated counterpart without invoking social burden. Generalisation is also narrow: the paper itself calls the reference “a sample of natural driving [in specific cities], not of all human driving” (Methods 4.3), while cross-source coverage and benchmark judgeability are uneven. I regard the monitoring formulation as promising, but the current evidence supports detection of model-relative, context-conditioned trajectory atypicality more strongly than online monitoring of social compliance.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Location: Sections 2.1 and 4.1; Fig. 2] — IPV lacks independent construct validation as a measure of social behaviour.** The manuscript calls IPV “a trajectory-derived measure of how an agent balances its own progress against the group's” (Section 1) and “one continuous, online-computable dimension of social interaction” (Section 2.1). Yet the candidates are generated from the authors' stipulated utility, “cos θk · (own-progress cost) + sin θk · (interaction cost),” and the observation model is a trajectory MSE likelihood with σ = 0.1 m (Methods 4.1). “Readability” is then the concentration of likelihood weights over these same candidates. Fig. 2 shows that the model discriminates real aligned interactions better than control pairings; it does not show that θ tracks a social preference rather than whichever longitudinal/lateral kinematics the chosen costs and reference geometry make identifiable. Even the reported online/offline agreement is explicitly “agreement between estimators, not recovery of a latent preference” (Section 2.1). At this journal's level, calibrating a human distribution of a model output does not by itself validate the output as social behaviour. **Remedy:** provide preregistered construct-validity tests against independent behavioural evidence: experimentally manipulated yielding/assertion under matched geometry; independent human judgements or choices; and robustness of verdicts to plausible alternative cost definitions, candidate grids, likelihood scales, reference geometries and inverse-planning models. If these tests are unavailable, consistently rename the quantity as model-relative trajectory atypicality and narrow the title and claims.

2. **[Location: Sections 2.3 and 3; Methods 4.3] — The claimed independence from the counterpart's hidden intention is not established by the ablation.** The paper concludes that “the monitor never has to read a hidden intention” (Section 1) and that “the current observable situation suffices” (Section 2.3). The actual ablation adds “the principal counterpart's current IPV,” yielding a “paired 90% interval-score difference −0.0002, case-clustered p = 0.86” (Methods 4.3). A noisy estimate from the same inverse-planning model is neither ground-truth intention nor a controlled variation in intention. No incremental interval-score gain could mean that the proxy is noisy, that the stated four categorical descriptors plus 22 numeric channels already contain behaviour through which intention is expressed, that the wide interval lacks sensitivity, or that intention matters only in ambiguous or partially observed states. It does not establish intention-independence, nor does it test flag stability or consequence prediction conditional on intent. **Remedy:** use scenarios in which counterpart goal/policy is known and varied while observable geometry and early kinematics are matched; report calibration, interval score, verdict changes and outcomes by intention condition. Otherwise replace the conclusion with the narrower result: adding this model's current counterpart-IPV estimate did not improve interval score in these data.

3. **[Location: Sections 2.4 and 2.6; Methods 4.3 and 4.5; Fig. 5] — The behavioural interpretation of the outcome signature is not separated from a mundane kinematic explanation.** The manuscript says assertive-side flags are “followed by a measurably tighter interaction for both vehicles” and calls this “a compression of ordinary interaction quality” (Section 2.4). However, the conditioning variables already include elapsed interaction time, both vehicles' velocity components and headings, relative distance, relative speed, closing rate, short-window kinematic summaries, current TTC and an anticipated post-encroachment proxy (Methods 4.3). The post-verdict outcomes are minimum TTC to the end of the run and the reactive simulated counterpart's speed variation/reduction over three seconds (Methods 4.5). These outcomes are natural continuations of the same coupled encounter dynamics. Reading them from “different sources and different quantities” does not make them independent evidence, despite the manuscript's claim that their agreement is “a convergence rather than a restatement” (Section 2.4). The timing placebo is useful, but it does not rule out residual encounter phase, baseline speed, initial closing trend or the TESS NG controller's ordinary response to proximity. The fact that emergency rates do not increase supports the authors' refusal to claim harm; it also makes “interaction quality” a normative label not measured here. **Remedy:** match or model flagged and within-range moments on full pre-verdict trajectories and scenario/run phase, show pre-trends, use one landmarked observation per encounter, and test robustness across counterpart controllers. Most importantly, add an independent human-dispreference endpoint (ratings, interventions, discomfort or predictability judgements). Without it, describe the result as a kinematic association, not reduced interaction quality or social burden.

4. **[Location: Sections 2.2 and 2.5; Methods 4.3, 4.4 and 4.6; Figs. 3c and 6] — The reference population and benchmark cannot carry the transfer language used in the paper.** Methods 4.3 states that the reference comes from “specific cities in the United States and Singapore” and “is a sample of natural driving there, not of all human driving.” Cross-source tests are weak: leave-one-source-out 90% coverage is 0.743 for Waymo and 0.750 for Lyft, and Argoverse-2 attains 0.900 with 44.3% abstention (Methods 4.4); Fig. 3c also reports negative held-out-source R² for Argoverse-2 and nuPlan. Against this, Section 2.5 says that a 4.7% flag rate for course drivers versus a 9.72% natural-driving rate shows that “carrying the reference across country, apparatus and task does not inflate its alarms.” Absence of inflation is only a one-sided observation: a much lower rate can equally indicate conservative test-site behaviour, protocol restriction or under-sensitivity. The human arm comprises “20 drivers × 15 scenarios,” all in “a single fixed sequence,” facing simulator-driven counterparts through a head-mounted display (Methods 4.5–4.6). These are valuable matched data, but not evidence of broad cultural, road-user or task transfer. **Remedy:** define the target human population; fully describe the 15 scenario templates and participant demographics/experience/recruitment; randomise or counterbalance order; report driver-, scenario- and subgroup-level calibration; and replicate at an independent site with real human counterparts and unseen interaction types. Until then, replace “the reference transfers across country, apparatus and task” (Section 1) with the narrower claim that alarms did not inflate in this one matched protocol.

5. **[Location: Methods 4.4–4.5; Sections 2.4–2.5; Fig. 6] — Per-moment weighting does not establish operational alarm behaviour or population differences.** The paper acknowledges that “Moments within an interaction are dependent,” that coverage is “not a per-interaction, sequential or conditional guarantee,” and that “every reported verdict and flag rate is per-moment”; episode-level false-alarm rate, detection delay and warning duration are “not evaluated” (Methods 4.4). It further states that consequence estimates are “weighted by moment rather than by scenario run” (Methods 4.5). At 10 Hz, adjacent flagged moments can represent one sustained event, and longer judgeable episodes contribute more heavily than shorter ones. Thus the reported 9.8% versus 4.7% and “2.1 times” difference (Section 2.5; 9.8/4.7 = 2.09) is a difference between moment-weighted exposure distributions, not necessarily between automated systems and human drivers as behavioural populations. The per-scenario statement “higher for the automated systems in 15 of 15 scenarios” (Section 2.5) is shown without uncertainty in Fig. 6c and does not address crossed dependence among systems/drivers and scenarios. **Remedy:** instantiate and freeze the persistence rule, then report alerts per run/interaction, duration, time to first alert and false-alert burden; analyse both equal-run and equal-agent estimands; and fit a crossed system/driver-by-scenario model or an equivalent hierarchical analysis. The population-comparison language should follow the estimand actually supported.

## 3. Minor issues

1. **[Location: Title; Abstract; Section 2.3]** The title promises “socially compliant” driving, whereas the paper's own supported endpoint is “conditional social atypicality, not inappropriateness.” The title should reflect atypicality unless normative validation is added.
2. **[Location: Methods note on scope, Section 4]** The manuscript defines online as non-anticipation and states that “end-to-end real-time operation on vehicle hardware is not evaluated.” Runtime, memory and latency measurements are needed before the monitor is described as deployable online rather than causally computable offline.
3. **[Location: Fig. 2b]** The panel explicitly says “uncertainty not shown” while using 0.30–0.31 versus 0.17 rad to support “Readable does not mean settled.” Show intervals, sample sizes and the clustered comparison.
4. **[Location: Section 2.3; Fig. 4a]** “Sharper” is relative: the manuscript reports that the conditioned 90% range still averages 1.87 rad over a 2.36-rad candidate span. State this prominently whenever discrimination is claimed, because the monitor remains coarse.
5. **[Location: Methods 4.6]** The human sample is described only as licensed, consenting and compensated. Report age, sex/gender, driving experience, recruitment source, exclusion criteria and prior exposure to the test apparatus.
6. **[Location: Fig. 6c]** The per-scenario plot states “uncertainty not shown.” Add intervals and identify how system-level and driver-level variation was aggregated within each scenario.
7. **[Location: Methods 4.2 and 4.4; Algorithm 2]** The operational definitions and thresholds for “Map-or-Role-Uncertain” and “Out-of-Distribution” are not presented with the same specificity as the near-uniform and support gates. Specify them and report their benchmark counts.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What evidence, independent of the current inverse-planning objective and candidate generator, shows that θ measures concern for another road user rather than a kinematic residual of that model?
2. How stable are frame-level estimates, readable moments and atypicality verdicts under alternative interaction-cost definitions, σ values, candidate grids and map/reference geometries?
3. What exactly was supplied as the counterpart-IPV ablation, with what measurement error and effective sample size, and how much power was available to detect a practically meaningful interval-score gain?
4. Can the authors produce matched cases with indistinguishable early observables but different known counterpart goals or policies? If not, on what basis is “hidden intention” ruled out rather than merely unnecessary for this predictor on these data?
5. After conditioning on the full pre-verdict kinematic history, scenario, controller state and encounter phase, do assertive-side flags still predict future TTC and counterpart speed changes? Please show balance and pre-trend diagnostics.
6. Why are TTC compression and speed adjustment termed “interaction quality” without a human rating, preference, comfort, predictability or burden measure?
7. What are the 15 scenario templates, their manoeuvre/priority/risk distribution, and the demographics and driving-experience distribution of the 20 human participants? Was fixed-order learning assessed?
8. What are the per-run and per-interaction alarm rates, alert durations and detection delays under a fixed persistence rule, and does the 9.8% versus 4.7% contrast survive equal weighting by run and by agent?
9. How do the authors reconcile the broad transfer wording with 0.743/0.750 leave-one-source-out coverage and the 44.3% Argoverse-2 abstention reported in Methods 4.4?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. Establish IPV construct validity with independent behavioural manipulation/measurement and model-specification robustness; otherwise recast the contribution as monitoring model-relative trajectory atypicality.
2. Replace the hidden-intention inference with a controlled, ground-truth intention/policy test, or narrow the claim to the reported counterpart-IPV ablation.
3. Reanalyse Fig. 5 using rigorous pre-verdict trajectory/phase matching and add an independent human-dispreference endpoint; until then remove “interaction quality” and social-burden interpretations.
4. Reframe the transfer claim and add an adequately described, counterbalanced and externally replicated human/reference evaluation with unseen interaction types.
5. Report episode/run-level operating characteristics under a frozen persistence rule and use crossed driver/system-by-scenario inference for human–AV comparisons.
6. Align the title, abstract and Discussion with the actual estimand and with the manuscript's own boundaries on real-time deployment, conditional coverage and pairwise vehicle-only scope.
7. Add the missing uncertainty, sample-size and gate-accounting details identified above.
