# Review of “Online monitoring of socially compliant autonomous driving”

## 1. Summary assessment

The manuscript proposes a selective, context-conditioned monitor that maps an online inverse-planning score (IPV) to a human reference interval and abstains when the score or reference is deemed unreliable. It then relates assertive-side flags to subsequent interaction outcomes and compares flag rates and consequence patterns between automated vehicles and human drivers in matched scenarios. The framing—separating atypicality, unreadability and harm—is useful. However, the principal statistical claims are not currently supported at the level required for *Nature Machine Intelligence*. The advertised conformal calibration is computed over hundreds of thousands of dependent moments although scenes, not moments, are the plausible exchangeability units; only marginal accepted-moment coverage is targeted; and severe held-out-source failures are already reported. Comparisons among naturalistic humans, matched-scenario humans and automated vehicles condition on different selective acceptance processes and use moment counts that obscure the much smaller crossed design of drivers/systems by 15 scenarios. The consequence analyses remain vulnerable to repeated-window dependence, state confounding, and mechanical coupling between the trajectory-derived flag and kinematic outcomes. Finally, a frozen battery does not resolve multiplicity or justify “no rise” claims. The core idea may be publishable, but it requires a cluster-valid calibration and inferential redesign, not merely clearer exposition.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Methods 4.4; Fig. 4] The stated conformal guarantee does not match the sampling unit.** The conformal radius is the order statistic of 455,723 accepted *moments*, and coverage is evaluated on 461,937 accepted moments. Yet the manuscript acknowledges that moments within an interaction are dependent and splits data by whole scenes. Scene separation prevents direct leakage, but it does not make moment-level nonconformity scores exchangeable: observations occur in highly correlated, unequal-sized clusters. Standard split-conformal finite-sample coverage therefore does not follow from the procedure as written. Calling the estimand “pointwise marginal coverage over accepted moments” states a target but does not establish the required exchangeability. This matters because “calibrated” is the central methodological claim, and the enormous moment count creates misleading apparent precision. The remedy is to define the stochastic sampling unit and target population explicitly, then calibrate and evaluate at an exchangeable cluster level (scene or interaction), or provide a valid hierarchical/cluster-conformal argument. At minimum, report scene-weighted random-moment coverage, interaction-level coverage, trajectory-wise simultaneous coverage, and cluster-based uncertainty. If the intended estimand is a size-biased random frame rather than a random interaction, that choice and its operational meaning must be explicit.

2. **[Results 2.3; Methods 4.4; Figs. 3c and 4] Marginal in-mixture coverage is inadequate for a situation-conditioned runtime claim, and the reported shift results reveal material failure.** At nominal 90% coverage, leave-one-source-out coverage is 74.3% for Waymo and 75.0% for Lyft; Argoverse-2 attains 90.0% only while abstaining on 44.3% of held-out moments. These are not small deviations. They show that the learned interval is neither distribution-free nor reliably transferable under source shift. More fundamentally, the monitor is marketed as a range “for the current situation”, but split conformal supplies only marginal coverage over accepted moments. Dominant, easy states can mask severe undercoverage in rare geometries, high-risk states, or particular sources—the cases where a runtime monitor is most consequential. The text says conditional state-group results are “reported as boundaries”, but no such numerical results are presented. The paper should sharply restrict “calibrated” to the in-mixture accepted-moment estimand unless it implements and validates group-, cluster- or shift-aware calibration. Coverage, interval width and abstention must be reported jointly by source, geometry, role, risk stratum, support distance and interaction phase, with uncertainty based on independent clusters. A target-domain calibration or a prospectively justified covariate-shift method is needed before transfer claims can be made.

3. **[Results 2.4–2.5; Methods 4.4–4.6; Fig. 6] Selective abstention makes the cross-population flag-rate comparison non-identifiable as currently reported.** The benchmark has 67,861 candidate moments but only 14,099 verdicts (20.8%), whereas the naturalistic result highlights a 5.08% *support* abstention after other readability filters. The paper does not provide a common flow accounting for active interaction, ego readability, counterpart stability, map/role certainty, human support and out-of-distribution rejection in each of the naturalistic-human, matched-human and automated-vehicle arms. A 9.845% versus 4.7% flag rate is conditional on being accepted by potentially different selection mechanisms. If automated systems enter different state mixtures, create longer interactions, or are readable/supportable at different times, the ratio need not reflect a difference in behavioural atypicality. Per-frame weighting also length-biases the estimand: systems or drivers that remain longer in a state contribute more observations. Abstention is part of monitor performance, not missing data that can be conditioned away. The authors should prespecify the estimand (random frame, random interaction, random run, random scenario, or standardized scenario-time), provide a complete reason-coded flow for each arm, compare arms on explicit common support, and report flag and abstention rates together. A run- or interaction-level primary analysis with equal scenario weighting is needed; per-moment results may be secondary.

4. **[Results 2.5; Methods 4.6; Fig. 6] The human-versus-automated comparison is analysed at the wrong effective sample size and lacks inferential support.** The design comprises 20 drivers and 19 automated systems crossed with only 15 scenario templates, not 15,102 and 14,099 independent observations. Aggregating frames and plotting 15 scenario means above parity does not address dependence induced by repeated measurements of the same driver/system, common scenario scripts, and unequal numbers of accepted frames. Fig. 6a explicitly omits uncertainty, and the claimed 2.1-fold difference has no cluster-valid interval or model. “15/15 scenarios above parity” describes these templates but is not evidence for a broader scenario population without a sampling model. The analysis should use the crossed design: a hierarchical model or multiway cluster bootstrap over driver/system and scenario, with run as the observational unit, scenario-standardized exposure, and confidence intervals for absolute and relative differences. The naturalistic 9.72% rate is largely a calibration check and should not be treated as a third exchangeable population. The matched-human 4.7% rate is also substantial overcoverage relative to a nominal 10% outside rate; “no alarm inflation” is only a one-sided statement and does not establish valid transfer. A prespecified equivalence/calibration margin is required.

5. **[Results 2.4 and 2.6; Methods 4.5; Fig. 5] The consequence analysis cannot distinguish a meaningful prognostic signature from state selection and trajectory coupling.** IPV, readability, situation variables and the flag are all derived from recent ego/counterpart trajectories; the outcomes are subsequent functions of closely related kinematics. Flagged frames cluster into stretches, their post-verdict windows overlap, and a single developing interaction can contribute many highly similar exposure–outcome pairs. Resampling 175 scenario runs addresses some within-run dependence, but not crossed system and scenario effects, unequal frame contributions, or confounding by interaction state. Reassigning whole flag sequences across runs is not an adequate placebo because it destroys alignment with the very state variables that generate both flags and outcomes. Temporal ordering alone does not make the association causal or even incrementally predictive. The manuscript is verbally cautious about causality, but phrases such as “locates the particular moments” and “the interaction that follows” imply more specificity than demonstrated. Analyses should collapse contiguous flags into prospectively defined episodes, avoid overlapping outcome windows, match or weight on all pre-flag state/risk variables within scenario, test pre-trends and lead/lag negative controls, and use system-by-scenario cluster-valid inference. If the intended claim is prediction, evaluate incremental out-of-sample prediction over the observable state alone; if it is causal, the present observational design is insufficient.

6. **[Results 2.4–2.6; Methods 4.5–4.6; Figs. 5 and 6] Multiplicity and absence-of-harm reasoning are not handled.** The “frozen battery” contains several distributional summaries, four ego emergency thresholds, four counterpart-response outcomes, three braking thresholds, both flag directions, a placebo, and a second human arm. Freezing a family prevents post-hoc threshold selection but does not control its family-wise error rate or false-discovery rate. The paper repeatedly treats a 95% interval excluding zero as “supported” and an interval including zero as no claim, then summarizes only the favourable subset. Moreover, “no rise”, “stay at or below”, and “without adding emergencies” are non-inferiority/equivalence claims; failure to observe an increase is not evidence of no meaningful increase. At the <3 s ego threshold, the reported interval extends to a +3.15 percentage-point increase, directly contradicting an unqualified “at or below” statement. The authors need a small set of prespecified primary endpoints, multiplicity-adjusted intervals or a hierarchical testing scheme, and domain-justified non-inferiority margins. All emergency claims should be rephrased as descriptive unless one-sided upper bounds exclude a meaningful increase after multiplicity adjustment.

7. **[Results 2.1–2.3; Methods 4.1–4.3; Figs. 1–4] The monitored estimand is an algorithm-derived score, not a validated human preference, and measurement invariance is untested.** The target used to build the human range is the same inverse-planning estimate, based on seven candidate values, a fixed Gaussian likelihood scale, a one-second window and a particular trajectory generator/reference geometry. Concentrated candidate weights show discrimination among those seven model outputs, not accuracy for a latent social preference. Agreement with an offline estimate from a closely related pipeline likewise does not establish construct validity. The reference interval therefore calibrates the distribution of a model-dependent score under selected human trajectories. It does not by itself license interpretations such as “weights the counterpart's cost less than humans do”. This is especially important when comparing human and automated controllers, for which model misspecification and trajectory-generator fit may differ. The paper should define the estimand as an online IPV *score* unless externally validated; report goodness-of-fit and residual diagnostics by domain; conduct sensitivity analyses over the candidate grid, likelihood scale, window, map reference and trajectory model; and quantify how score uncertainty affects range membership. Stronger preference language requires validation against controlled incentives/interventions or independent human judgements.

8. **[Results 2.1–2.5; Methods 4.1–4.6] The confirmatory and modelling protocol is not specified sufficiently to audit the statistical claims.** The conditional-quantile model class, loss, hyperparameters, feature encoding, missing-data handling, tuning criterion, fold use and model-selection path are absent. The manuscript repeatedly says thresholds and batteries were “frozen”, but does not identify the preregistration, timestamp, primary estimands, stopping rules or deviations. Fig. 2 says the confirmatory split remained sealed, yet the manuscript does not clearly state which displayed results constitute the one-time confirmatory evaluation. Case counts also change from 38,228 interactions overall to 26,828 reference cases without a consolidated flow. For a paper whose contribution is auditability, these omissions are consequential. The authors should provide a complete analysis provenance table, a participant/scene/case/moment flow, exact model specification, all tuning decisions, the role of every split, and the unedited prespecified analysis plan. Confirmatory results must be clearly separated from exploratory analyses.

## 3. Minor issues

1. Report cluster-valid confidence intervals for achieved coverage, interval width, abstention, the 20.9% variance-explained estimate, every flag rate and the 2.1-fold ratio. Large frame counts should never substitute for the number of scenes, interactions, runs, drivers or systems.
2. Replace “coverage stays at nominal” in Fig. 4 with language that reflects empirical, in-mixture accepted-moment coverage and its uncertainty.
3. The claim in Fig. 2a that the reading becomes readable “only” during a real interaction is stronger than the control results: the different-partner control remains shifted in the same direction. Define the contrast and report paired differences among conditions.
4. Explain how conformal widening interacts with the bounded IPV scale and whether intervals are clipped. Clipping can change width and coverage interpretation near ±π/2.
5. Define precisely how the 20.9% out-of-fold variance explained is computed from a quantile model and provide its fold- and source-specific values.
6. “Shared consequence signature” should require a formal cross-arm interaction or equivalence analysis; visually similar point estimates are not sufficient.
7. Report ethics approval, consent, eligibility, recruitment, demographic information, run order/randomisation and missing-run handling for the 20-driver study.
8. Algorithm 2 includes persistence tuning and planner-facing signals, but neither is evaluated. These should be removed from the validated monitor claim or assessed prospectively.
9. Clarify whether a “candidate moment”, “anchor row”, “readable moment”, “accepted moment” and “judgeable moment” are distinct, and use one denominator table consistently throughout.
10. The descriptive case in Fig. ED2 is selected from only two eligible runs after several outcome-based display filters. Its title and main-text reference should continue to make clear that it is not representative evidence.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What is the exact exchangeability unit for the conformal argument? Please provide either a proof for the accepted, clustered moment protocol or a cluster-valid replacement.
2. How many independent scenes and interactions—not frames—enter each train, guard, calibration and test fold, by source? How variable are cluster sizes?
3. What are the 80%, 90% and 95% coverage, interval widths and abstention rates within each prespecified source, geometry, role, risk, interaction-phase and support-distance stratum?
4. What exact conditional-quantile model, feature set, hyperparameters, tuning procedure and random seeds were used? Were any choices inspected on the test or confirmatory data?
5. What were the total candidate counts and reason-specific abstention counts for naturalistic humans, matched-scenario humans and automated vehicles under the identical pipeline?
6. What is the formal target of the 2.1-fold comparison: a random moment, run, scenario, driver/system, or standardized scenario-time? What is its confidence interval under crossed driver/system-by-scenario dependence?
7. Why is 4.7% outside a nominal 90% range interpreted as successful transfer rather than material overcoverage or selection? What equivalence margin was prespecified?
8. How many non-overlapping assertive episodes do the 472 flagged AV moments represent, and how sensitive are all Fig. 5 estimates to one observation per episode, one per run, or equal run weighting?
9. Does the flag improve held-out prediction of each consequence beyond the full observable situation vector used to construct the range? Please report incremental predictive performance.
10. Which hypotheses and thresholds were primary, what multiplicity procedure was prespecified, and where is the dated protocol? Were the human-arm analyses specified before any human-arm outcomes were examined?
11. How stable are verdicts under plausible changes to the seven-point candidate grid, σ = 0.1 m, window length, trajectory generator and static map reference?
12. Are estimator fit and readability comparable between human and automated trajectories after conditioning on scenario and state, or could domain-specific model misspecification explain part of the flag-rate difference?
13. For outcomes whose window begins at the verdict time, is the sample at the verdict timestamp used in both exposure construction and outcome calculation? Please provide a strict no-overlap sensitivity analysis.
14. How were repeated runs, missing runs and ordering/learning effects handled in the 20-driver and 19-system matched-scenario design?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Rebuild calibration around a valid independent unit.** Use cluster/hierarchical conformal calibration or justify an alternative formally; report cluster-weighted marginal, subgroup and trajectory-wise coverage with uncertainty.
2. **Make distribution shift and selective abstention first-class outcomes.** Provide full reason-coded flows by population and state, common-support analyses, coverage–width–abstention trade-offs, and appropriately restricted transfer claims.
3. **Reanalyse the human-versus-automated comparison using the crossed run-level design.** Standardize over scenarios and report multiway-cluster or hierarchical uncertainty for absolute and relative flag differences.
4. **Redesign the consequence analysis at the episode/run level.** Remove overlapping repeated-frame pseudo-replication, adjust for pre-verdict state, use negative controls and incremental held-out prediction, and keep all conclusions explicitly associational.
5. **Institute multiplicity and non-inferiority discipline.** Identify primary endpoints, adjust the frozen family, specify meaningful margins, and retract “no rise/at or below” statements not supported by adjusted upper bounds.
6. **Validate and appropriately name the measured construct.** Treat IPV as a model-derived score unless independent construct validation is supplied; add domain-specific fit and hyperparameter-sensitivity analyses.
7. **Provide a fully auditable analysis specification.** Document model details, data flow, split governance, preregistration and deviations, with confirmatory results clearly separated from exploratory work.
