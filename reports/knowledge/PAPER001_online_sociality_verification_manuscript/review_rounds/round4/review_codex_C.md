# Summary assessment

This paper proposes a frame-level monitor of “conditional social atypicality”. A likelihood-based inverse-planning estimator produces an interaction preference value (IPV); the monitor abstains when that estimate is deemed unreadable or the situation lacks human support; otherwise it compares the estimate with a situation-conditioned human reference interval calibrated using split conformal prediction. Naturalistic data are used to build the reference, and matched real-vehicle runs are used to relate assertive-side flags to subsequent margins and counterpart control responses and to compare automated vehicles with human drivers.

The manuscript does not yet support its central methodological claims. The calibrated object is a highly dependent, moment-weighted model output rather than an independently validated preference construct. The usual split-conformal guarantee does not follow from treating hundreds of thousands of correlated frames as calibration observations, and coverage is only empirical after selection. Severe leave-one-source-out failures, high benchmark abstention, and unequal selection across populations undermine the transfer claims. Real-vehicle inference further ignores crossed dependence by scenario, driver/system and persistent flag stretch, while many conclusions rely on unadjusted endpoint-wise intervals. The consequence results are observational and partly mechanically tied to the state used to define a flag. Substantial reanalysis and, for construct validity, additional validation are required.

# Major weaknesses

1. **[Locations: Sections 2.1, 4.1–4.3; Fig. 2] — The monitored quantity is not independently validated as a social preference.** The target used to learn the human range is itself the output of the same seven-candidate inverse-planning model later applied to automated and human runs. Concentration of candidate weights shows that the chosen model distinguishes its candidates; it does not show that the resulting IPV is accurate, identifiable, socially meaningful or comparable across sensing and map domains. Likewise, the reported agreement between online and offline estimates is agreement between closely related estimators, not validation against a latent preference. The fixed cost form, candidate grid, likelihood scale (`sigma = 0.1 m`), one-second window and map reference can all determine both the apparent human range and who is flagged. The threshold `max weight > 0.20` is called a reliability rule without any calibration of that quantity to estimation error. This is decisive because conformal calibration can calibrate coverage for this proxy while saying nothing about coverage of human social preference. The authors should provide recovery experiments with known generative preferences, controlled or annotated interactions with an external criterion, and sensitivity analyses over the grid, cost specification, likelihood scale, window length, solver and reference geometry. At minimum, claims should be reframed as monitoring atypicality of an IPV-model output, and estimator uncertainty should be propagated rather than replaced by a hard concentration gate.

2. **[Locations: Sections 2.3 and 4.4; Fig. 4] — The conformal construction does not have the usual stated sampling justification, and “certifies” is too strong.** The conformal quantile is computed from 455,723 accepted calibration moments, although adjacent 10-Hz windows within an interaction overlap and are strongly dependent. Splitting whole scenes prevents direct train/test leakage, but it does not make individual moment scores exchangeable. Consequently, the standard finite-sample split-conformal coverage result does not apply to the moment-level order statistic used here. The paper acknowledges that it lacks per-interaction, sequential and conditional coverage, but still calls the interval “calibrated”, says the conformal step “certifies” the quantile model, and emphasizes deviations from nominal as small as 0.03 percentage points without cluster-based uncertainty. Those numerical discrepancies are not interpretable against a sample size of 461,937 correlated moments. The remedy is a conformal procedure whose calibration unit matches the independent sampling unit—for example, scene/interaction-block scores, hierarchical conformal calibration or an explicitly defined random-scene/random-time estimand with appropriate weights. The paper should report uncertainty for achieved coverage using independent clusters, distributions of coverage across interactions and scenes, and coverage by source, role, geometry and risk/support strata. If the authors retain the present method, it must be presented as empirical same-mixture calibration rather than a distribution-free guarantee.

3. **[Locations: Sections 2.3–2.5 and 4.4; Figs. 4 and 6] — Coverage after abstention is being conflated with robustness under distribution shift.** Calibration is only over accepted natural-driving moments. On the natural test fold the support gate rejects 5.08%, but on the real-vehicle benchmark only 14,099 of 67,861 candidate moments (20.8%) receive a verdict, and 36 of 267 runs receive none because of missing human support. Conditional flag rates among these selected moments are therefore not directly comparable unless acceptance is shown to select the same target population in natural-driving, matched-human and automated-vehicle groups. This is especially important because readability and support can depend on behaviour. The leave-one-source-out results directly show lack of transportability: nominal 90% coverage becomes 0.743 for Waymo and 0.750 for Lyft, while nuPlan overcovers at 0.990 and Argoverse-2 reaches 0.900 only with 44.3% abstention. Against that evidence, a 4.7% outside rate in the matched human arm is not proof that the reference “transfers”; it can equally indicate substantial overcoverage on the selected subset. The authors should report the complete acceptance/abstention cascade, with denominators and reason codes, separately by population, source, scenario, driver/system and nominal level; provide risk–coverage curves; and define the target distribution for every flag-rate comparison. External validation should use source- or group-aware calibration or a shift-aware conformal method evaluated on an honest held-out domain. Coverage and selectivity must be analysed jointly, not as a coverage number conditional on a changing gate.

4. **[Locations: Sections 2.4–2.5 and 4.5–4.6; Figs. 5 and 6] — The real-vehicle estimands and dependence structure are not handled adequately.** Verdicts occur every 0.1 s, adjacent one-second estimation windows overlap, consecutive flagged moments form stretches, and post-verdict outcome windows overlap. Those observations are nested in runs, while runs are crossed by 15 reused scenario templates and 19 automated systems or 20 human drivers. Resampling 175 runs addresses within-run repetition but not dependence induced by the same system/driver across scenarios or by the same scenario across systems/drivers. It also does not make 472 flagged moments equivalent to 472 independent events. Fig. 6a and c show no uncertainty, and “15/15 scenarios above parity” is descriptive, not inference for either a scenario or system population. Moment-weighted rates can additionally change when groups differ in judgeable duration or acceptance. The manuscript needs an explicit estimand: for example, the flag probability for a uniformly sampled run, scenario, system/driver or judgeable moment. Persistent flags should be collapsed into pre-defined alert episodes or analysed as recurrent longitudinal events. Inference should account for crossed scenario and system/driver effects, using multiway cluster bootstrap, a hierarchical model or randomization inference justified by the assignment design. Report per-run and per-subject/system summaries, effective numbers of independent alert stretches, uncertainty for all human–AV contrasts, recruitment and ordering/randomization details, and reasons why 267 rather than `19 × 15 = 285` automated runs enter.

5. **[Locations: Equation 3, Sections 2.2–2.3 and 4.3; Fig. 3] — “Situation” is not separated cleanly from the behaviour being judged.** The conditioning vector includes current progress, relative kinematics and online risk proxies. These can be consequences of the ego’s behaviour and of the counterpart’s response at or before the same moment from which IPV is inferred. Availability at decision time establishes non-anticipation, but not that the variables are pre-behaviour context. Conditioning on behaviour-generated state can normalize part of an assertive manoeuvre into its own reference range, induce collider bias, and make “same situation” a different estimand from the counterfactual comparison suggested by the prose. The claim that no hidden-intention estimate or temporal history is needed is also not established by a non-significant interval-score difference (`p = 0.86`); lack of detected improvement is not an equivalence result, and current kinematics may already encode both agents’ histories. In Fig. 3a, realized post-encroachment time is explicitly post-outcome and therefore cannot establish a prospective risk-conditioned relation, even if the display is labelled descriptive. The authors should list every feature and its temporal offset, provide a causal/time-order diagram, define which state variables may be downstream of the action under evaluation, and repeat the analysis with map/role and suitably lagged pre-decision covariates. An equivalence margin and confidence interval are required before claiming that counterpart IPV or ego history adds no meaningful value.

6. **[Locations: Sections 2.4, 2.6 and 4.5; Fig. 5] — The consequence language exceeds what the observational design identifies.** The authors appropriately deny a causal claim, yet “interaction quality compresses”, “the counterpart absorbs” and “the interaction that follows” give the results a directional and normative interpretation. A flag is a function of the immediately preceding ego trajectory and contemporaneous relative state; future TTC and counterpart speed are continuations of that same dynamical episode. Shared scenario difficulty, prior counterpart response, baseline speed and persistence of the interaction can generate the association without the flagged behaviour causing any consequence. The open-ended TTC window also makes outcomes depend on remaining run length. The timing placebo gives `p = 0.0199` from only 200 reassignments, whereas the case-level label permutation gives `p = 0.1493`; the manuscript does not explain why the former, rather than the latter, validates the emphasized result. The remedy is a pre-defined primary outcome and time horizon, adjustment or matching on pre-flag trajectory and baseline outcome state, lead/lag and pre-trend analyses, negative-control times, and episode-level inference. Until then, the paper should describe “associations with subsequent margins and control variation”, not quality, burden or response. Claims of no increase across the tested emergency-threshold family require a non-inferiority margin and simultaneous uncertainty; pointwise intervals below zero do not establish a family-wide conclusion at every threshold.

7. **[Locations: Sections 2.2–2.5 and 4.3–4.6; Figs. 2–6] — The analysis battery has no adequate multiplicity discipline.** The paper evaluates three coverage levels, several risk/geometry partitions, multiple reference models and ablations, six level–window TTC combinations, four nested ego emergency thresholds, three braking thresholds, four counterpart outcomes, two atypicality directions, multiple placebos and a second human arm. Intervals are explicitly “per endpoint”, yet prose promotes whichever endpoints exclude the null as “supported” and then generalizes across “every supported threshold”. Freezing a battery prevents undisclosed threshold selection but does not control family-wise error or the false discovery rate, and nested thresholds do not eliminate multiplicity. The authors should identify primary, secondary and exploratory endpoints with a dated analysis plan; give simultaneous intervals or an appropriate hierarchical testing procedure for each claimed family; report adjusted and unadjusted results; and avoid defining evidential support by the same pointwise interval used to make the claim. More permutation draws and Monte Carlo uncertainty are needed for a reported probability near 0.02.

8. **[Locations: Sections 2.1, 2.4, 3 and 4.2–4.6; Figs. 2 and 4] — A retrospective per-frame score has not yet been validated as an online monitor.** The most readable estimates move by about 0.30 rad between consecutive frames, only 20.8% of candidate benchmark moments receive a verdict, and the deployment persistence layer is explicitly unevaluated. Reporting that 86.5% of runs receive at least one verdict does not establish useful warning availability, stability or timeliness. Repeated per-frame testing also means a nominal 10% outside probability cannot be interpreted as a 10% episode-level false-alarm probability. End-to-end computation on vehicle hardware is outside scope. For an NMI claim of online monitoring, the authors should freeze and evaluate the sequential interface: alert-episode definition, run-level false-alarm probability, detection delay, warning duration/chatter, coverage after repeated use, availability by scenario phase, and computational latency. Otherwise the contribution should be described more narrowly as retrospective frame-level scoring with online-available inputs.

9. **[Locations: Sections 4.3–4.4 and Algorithms 1–2] — Essential modelling and split details are absent from the paper.** “Conditional quantile regression” is not enough to reproduce or audit the reference: the model class, architecture or basis, exact covariates and encodings, loss, regularization, hyperparameter search, source and moment weighting, quantile-level coupling, tuning folds, missing-data rules and model-selection chronology are not specified. It is also unclear whether the confirmatory/test data remained sealed while the many gates, ablations and display choices were developed. Code release after publication does not replace a self-contained methods description. The revision should provide these specifications, a full flow diagram from 38,228 cases to every split and endpoint denominator, and a dated account of what was frozen before each test set and human-arm outcome was accessed.

# Minor issues

1. Fig. 4 should show cluster-based confidence intervals for both width reduction and achieved coverage; differences of hundredths of a percentage point should not be foregrounded without them.
2. Define “case”, “scene”, “interaction”, “scenario”, “run”, “anchor”, “candidate moment”, “readable moment”, “accepted moment” and “judgeable moment” in one table and map each figure’s `n` to one of these units.
3. Report frame-rate harmonization and down-sampling across all four naturalistic sources; a moment-weighted reference can otherwise be source-frame-rate weighted.
4. Explain and tabulate the exclusions from 38,228 interaction cases to 26,828 cases in the anchor ledger and from 519 assertive flags to the 472 ego-margin and 469 counterpart-response observations.
5. Specify the bootstrap interval type, random seeds and handling of resamples with no flagged events.
6. Fig. 2a needs a mathematical definition and units for “change in how sharply the reading is identified”; the four sample sizes are not directly comparable without the eligibility rule.
7. In Fig. 6b, replace “an asterisk marks an interval excluding no difference” with the precise null value for each ratio and provide the intervals numerically.
8. The text promises total abstention and reason distributions, but only selected aggregate counts are visible. Provide the complete table for naturalistic, matched-human and automated populations.
9. Report ethics approval, informed consent, driver inclusion/exclusion criteria, demographics, compensation and safety procedures for the human study.
10. Correct the Extended Data title “changes width with with the situation”.

# Questions to the authors

1. What population-level quantity is the principal estimand: a random accepted frame, random interaction, random run, random scenario, random driver/system, or a standardized combination? Why is moment weighting appropriate for each headline comparison?
2. What are the independent sampling units in the naturalistic calibration/test folds, and what argument makes the pooled moment-level conformal scores exchangeable?
3. What are the numbers of scenes and interactions—not frames—in each train, guard, calibration and test fold, and what are cluster-based confidence intervals for the reported 80%, 90% and 95% coverages?
4. What exact quantile-regression model and tuning protocol produced the conditional intervals? Was the test fold inaccessible until the model, support gate, candidate grid, thresholds and all ablations were frozen?
5. Which components of `z_t` use measurements at time `t`, which use the one-second estimation window, and which can be affected by the ego action whose IPV is being judged?
6. How do acceptance rates and abstention reasons differ among natural-driving humans, matched-scenario humans and automated systems, overall and within each of the 15 scenarios?
7. How many distinct assertive alert stretches, systems, scenario templates and runs contribute to each Fig. 5 endpoint? How much do results change after retaining one pre-defined index time per stretch or one summary per run?
8. How were systems and drivers assigned to scenario order and counterpart injections? What runs are missing from the nominal `19 × 15` and `20 × 15` matrices, and were any exclusions related to observed behaviour or outcomes?
9. What uncertainty interval accompanies the 2.1-fold human–AV flag-rate contrast after standardizing across scenarios and accounting jointly for repeated drivers/systems and scenarios?
10. What equivalence margin was specified for the assertion that counterpart IPV and ego history provide “no measurable value”? Please report confidence intervals for the paired interval-score differences, not only a null-hypothesis `p` value.
11. Which consequence endpoint, time window and atypicality direction were primary? How were multiplicity and the discrepancy between the timing-placebo result (`p = 0.0199`) and case-label permutation (`p = 0.1493`) handled in the analysis plan?
12. What external evidence establishes that IPV differences correspond to human-perceived assertiveness, accommodation or social acceptability rather than only to the chosen inverse-planning cost parameter?

# Prioritised revision requests

1. Rebuild the calibration analysis at an independent scene/interaction unit, report cluster uncertainty and subgroup/source coverage, and jointly analyse coverage with abstention under shift.
2. Establish or sharply limit the construct validity of IPV through external validation and comprehensive estimator sensitivity analyses.
3. Reanalyse the matched benchmark with a declared estimand, episode-level exposures, scenario-by-driver/system dependence, standardized denominators and complete uncertainty for human–AV contrasts.
4. Pre-specify an endpoint hierarchy, apply simultaneous/multiplicity-aware inference, and replace no-harm/no-rise language with formal non-inferiority analysis or bounded descriptive statements.
5. Separate pre-decision context from behaviour-generated covariates and perform lagged-state, baseline-adjusted, lead/lag and negative-control analyses.
6. Evaluate the sequential alert interface and risk–coverage/availability trade-off, or narrow the paper’s claim from an online monitor to frame-level retrospective scoring using online-available inputs.
7. Supply full model, split, exclusion, human-study and ethics details sufficient to reproduce every denominator and verify what was frozen before confirmatory evaluation.

# Acceptance probability

- **As submitted:** 5%.
- **Assuming a competent major revision:** 45%. This would require more than textual clarification: the conformal and benchmark analyses must be rebuilt at defensible sampling units, and the IPV construct needs independent validation or materially narrower claims.
