# Reviewer C report

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes a frame-level monitor that estimates an interaction preference value (IPV), abstains when the estimate or human support is inadequate, and compares accepted estimates with situation-conditioned human reference intervals. It then relates assertive-side flags to subsequent vehicle-interaction summaries and compares flag rates between automated systems and humans in matched scenarios.

The central statistical claim is not yet established at the level required for a runtime monitor. The stated conformal estimand is marginal coverage over accepted moments, but calibration uses many dependent moments as calibration units; it provides neither scene-level finite-sample validity nor conditional validity for the “current situation.” This limitation is consequential under shift: held-out-source 90% coverage is only 0.743 and 0.750 for two sources, while the support gate does not intercept the failure. On the real-vehicle benchmark, only 20.8% of candidate moments receive verdicts, and comparisons are then made among selected, repeated moments. The reported 2.1-fold human–AV difference lacks an appropriate crossed scenario/driver/system analysis. Consequence results remain vulnerable to phase and kinematic confounding, overlapping windows, and uncorrected endpoint multiplicity. The authors appropriately deny causal and harm claims, but stronger operational statements recur. I would require a substantial statistical re-analysis and narrower claims.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Results 2.3; Methods 4.4; Fig. 4] — The conformal construction does not support the advertised calibration interpretation.** The conformal radius is the rank of 455,723 accepted *moment-level* scores, although moments are repeatedly sampled within interaction cases and scenes. Splitting by whole scene prevents direct leakage, but it does not make the hundreds of thousands of calibration scores exchangeable calibration units; long scenes also receive more weight in the target than short scenes. The manuscript ultimately calls the result “empirical and marginal,” which is accurate, but elsewhere calls the range “calibrated” and interprets it for the current situation. Fig. 4b is therefore a test-fold calibration curve for a length-weighted accepted-moment distribution, not a distribution-free runtime guarantee. Moreover, marginal 90% coverage can coexist with arbitrarily poor coverage in particular situations, roles, sources or trajectories—the very levels at which the monitor is interpreted. This is a central NMI-level issue because conformal terminology appears to provide assurance that the analysis does not deliver. The remedy is to define the sampling unit and target population precisely; calibrate and evaluate using independent clusters (or a justified cluster-/hierarchical-conformal construction); give uncertainty over cluster-level coverage; and report coverage conditional on prespecified role, geometry, risk, source, range-width and support strata. If only empirical frame-marginal calibration is retained, all guarantee-like language should be removed.

2. **[Results 2.3–2.5; Methods 4.4; Figs. 4 and 6] — Distribution shift and selective abstention are not controlled by the proposed support gate.** At nominal 90% coverage, leave-one-source-out coverage is 0.743 for Waymo (143,380/193,096) and 0.750 for Lyft (68,270/91,069), despite the gate; Argoverse-2 attains 0.900 only with 44.3% abstention. These are large failures, not a minor boundary. On the real-vehicle benchmark, verdicts are returned for only 14,099/67,861 candidate moments (20.8%). The claim that the reference “survives” transfer because matched humans are flagged on 713/15,102 judgeable moments (4.7%), less than the natural-driving rate of 9.72%, is not a calibration test: a low conditional alarm rate can arise from over-wide intervals, covariate shift, or preferential rejection. It also does not reveal performance on the 79.2% of automated candidate moments rejected by the two gates. This matters because selective systems must be assessed jointly on risk and coverage, not on accepted-case accuracy alone. The paper should report, separately by source, scenario and human/AV arm, the full candidate-to-readable-to-supported-to-verdict flow, interval widths, flag rates using both accepted and all-candidate denominators, and risk–coverage curves. A target-domain calibration set or a shift-aware method with a genuinely held-out target evaluation is required before cross-country/apparatus transfer can be claimed.

3. **[Results 2.4; Methods 4.4 and Algorithm 2] — A per-frame reference interval has not been validated as an online alerting system.** At a nominal 90% level, roughly 10% of accepted human moments are expected to lie outside the interval by construction. Repeated, autocorrelated testing across frames can therefore make the probability of at least one flag per interaction or run much larger than 10%, even under an appropriate human process. The manuscript explicitly leaves persistence rules, episode-level false-alarm rate, detection delay and warning duration unevaluated. Consequently, the current output is a frame-level atypicality score with abstention, not yet a validated runtime monitor or planner-facing alert. This distinction is operationally decisive. The authors should freeze a sequential decision rule and evaluate interaction-/run-level false-alarm probability, alert duration, time to first alert, detection delay, and coverage under temporal dependence. Alternatively, they should consistently narrow the paper to a frame-level diagnostic and remove deployment claims.

4. **[Results 2.5; Methods 4.6; Fig. 6] — The human–AV comparison has no adequate estimand or inferential analysis.** The reported 2.1-fold difference is the ratio of moment-weighted conditional rates, 9.8% versus 4.7%, after readability and support selection. Moments are nested within runs, runs within drivers or automated systems, and both are crossed with 15 fixed scenarios. Different systems can also contribute different numbers of judgeable moments, so the result need not equal a driver-/system-level or scenario-level contrast. Fig. 6a and c explicitly omit uncertainty. “15/15 scenarios above parity” describes this scenario set but does not quantify the population contrast; nor does separate significance of consequence ratios establish that humans and AVs have the “same” signature. The nominal design also implies 19×15=285 AV cells, whereas only 267 AV runs are reported, and the manuscript does not give the corresponding human run flow. Remedy: predefine whether the target is a random moment, run, system/driver or scenario; analyse run-level rates with a crossed hierarchical model or a resampling scheme respecting system/driver and scenario; report absolute and relative effects with intervals; disclose missing cells and selection rates; and test AV–human heterogeneity or equivalence directly for the consequence signature.

5. **[Results 2.4; Methods 4.5; Fig. 5] — The consequence analysis does not isolate the information added by the flag from the evolving interaction state.** The flag, situation features, TTC, speed change and braking are all functions of the same evolving trajectories. Flagged moments can occur at different interaction phases and baseline risk states than within-range moments; multiple flagged and control moments from the same run generate heavily overlapping post-verdict windows. Cluster bootstrapping repairs neither this time-varying confounding nor the induced outcome duplication. “Within frozen situation cells” is insufficiently specified to assess adjustment or overlap. The timing placebo gives empirical p=0.0199 from only 200 reassignments, whereas a case-level label permutation on the same battery gives p=0.1493; selecting the former as the relevant success does not resolve the fragility. These analyses can support a descriptive conditional association, but not the stronger statements that the flag “locates” consequential moments or that the subsequent compression is specifically attributable to atypicality rather than current kinematics and phase. A defensible revision should use a landmark/first-eligible-moment design or non-overlapping risk sets, match or weight on prespecified pre-flag histories, show overlap and balance, adjust for pre-period outcomes, include negative-control times/outcomes, and present sensitivity analyses. Both permutation results should be foregrounded.

6. **[Abstract; Results 2.4 and 2.6; Fig. 5] — Multiplicity and “no harm” are handled by significance-dependent wording rather than a valid inferential target.** The manuscript reports four nested TTC thresholds, three nested braking thresholds, four counterpart responses, several coverage levels, and six level–window combinations. Calling a threshold “supported” exactly when its per-endpoint 95% interval excludes zero and then claiming that emergency events are rarer “at every supported threshold” is circular; freezing the battery prevents undisclosed threshold selection but does not control family-wise error or false discovery. Conversely, a non-significant difference does not establish no increase: for TTC <3 s the interval extends to +3.15 percentage points. To claim absence of increased emergency risk, the authors need prespecified non-inferiority margins and multiplicity-adjusted one-sided upper bounds (or simultaneous confidence bands). Otherwise, one primary threshold/outcome should be designated and the remaining battery labelled exploratory. The abstract and section titles should not imply a bounded harm result beyond what those bounds establish.

7. **[Results 2.1–2.3; Methods 4.1–4.3] — Statistical calibration of an estimator output is being conflated with validation of the social construct.** The target variable is the output of the same seven-candidate inverse-planning model, with a fixed likelihood scale (σ=0.1 m) and a weak concentration gate (maximum weight >0.20), not an observed preference. Agreement between causal-prefix and offline versions of the estimator and the control comparisons in Fig. 2 address estimator consistency or discriminability, not whether IPV represents perceived social compliance. Conformal coverage of these outputs can be excellent even if the model is misspecified. Likewise, failure of counterpart-IPV or self-history inputs to improve interval score (p=0.86 for one ablation) does not establish that the observable situation “suffices” or that partner information is redundant; that requires an equivalence margin, adequate power and model-class robustness. At minimum, the paper needs sensitivity to candidate-grid resolution, likelihood scale, window length and gate threshold, with estimator uncertainty propagated into the reference decision. External construct validation against blinded human judgements or controlled behavioural manipulations is needed for the broader “social compliance” interpretation; absent that, the title and claims should consistently say “model-estimated conditional behavioural atypicality.”

## 3. Minor issues

1. The flow from 38,228 interaction cases to 26,828 cases in the anchor ledger, 4,497,368 anchor rows, 2,442,625 human-reference rows, and the four splits should be shown in one diagram with every exclusion and denominator.
2. Report numbers of scenes—not only moments and interaction cases—in train, guard, calibration and test, because scenes are the stated split unit.
3. Fig. 3c needs uncertainty for held-out-source R², and the text should not treat four source point estimates as stable transfer diagnostics.
4. The asserted event-level partner correlation needs its coefficient, interval, unit of analysis and adjustment specification.
5. Fig. 2b, Fig. 2c and Fig. 6a/c omit uncertainty. “Uncertainty not shown” is not adequate for quantities used in central claims.
6. Define the conditional-quantile learner, hyperparameter selection, loss, feature transformations, missing-data handling and non-crossing rearrangement in the Methods rather than deferring the operative model to released code.
7. Specify bootstrap interval type, finite-cluster correction, strata, and how resamples handle runs contributing zero events. For the 200-draw placebo, report the exceedance count and Monte Carlo interval for the p-value.
8. The global 95% range covering 100% of a seven-point candidate span may be a grid-boundary artefact. Show that the narrowing and flag rates are stable to a denser candidate grid and to the declared [−π/2, π/2] domain.
9. Explain the complete flow from 267 benchmark runs to 175 non-scripted runs with recorded outcomes and the additional 27 scripted-counterpart exclusions; compare included and excluded runs.
10. “The remaining 79.1% is not recoverable from the situation description” overstates 1−R². It is residual variance under this fitted model and feature set, not an irreducible quantity.
11. Use distinct terms for model-support acceptance and statistical evidence; the overloaded word “supported” in Fig. 5 invites confusion despite the caption’s clarification.
12. The bootstrap CIs in Fig. 3a and the several descriptive subgroup contrasts should either enter the multiplicity hierarchy or be clearly labelled exploratory.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What exact population and sampling unit define each primary estimand: accepted frame, candidate frame, interaction, run, scenario, driver/system, or scene?
2. How many independent scenes are in each natural-driving split, and why is ranking all within-scene calibration moments valid for the claimed conformal procedure?
3. What are empirical coverage and interval width by source, role, geometry, risk-proxy bin, interaction phase, support-distance bin and interval-width bin?
4. Why does the support/OOD gate permit 90% coverage to fall to 74.3% and 75.0% in held-out sources, and what diagnostic is meant to protect deployment under a new source?
5. For humans and AVs separately, how many candidate moments, readable moments, support-accepted moments, judgeable runs and total runs occur in each scenario? What are the reason-specific abstention rates?
6. Which 18 of the nominal 285 AV system–scenario cells are missing, and are any human driver–scenario cells missing? Were missingness and failed runs related to scenario difficulty?
7. Is 2.1× intended as a moment-weighted descriptive ratio or as an estimate for a population of systems and drivers? What crossed-cluster model supports the latter interpretation?
8. What formal test or equivalence analysis supports the statement that the human and AV arms have the “same consequence signature”?
9. What are the frozen situation cells in Methods 4.5, how were they chosen, how many observations/runs populate each, and how is lack of overlap handled?
10. Why is the whole-trajectory timing permutation considered confirmatory when the case-label permutation is not significant? What were the exact permutation nulls and exceedance counts?
11. Which emergency endpoint and threshold were primary, what multiplicity procedure was prespecified, and what non-inferiority margin would rule out a practically important increase?
12. What are the run-level probabilities of at least one flag, the number and duration of flag episodes, and the results under a frozen persistence rule?
13. How were the quantile model, support-distance threshold, IPV likelihood scale, candidate grid and readability threshold selected without using confirmatory outcomes?
14. What external evidence validates IPV as perceived social preference rather than merely a stable inverse-planning score?
15. Was the analysis plan time-stamped or registered, and which analyses, endpoints and figures were specified before opening the confirmatory and outcome data?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Rebuild the calibration analysis around independent clusters and explicit estimands.** Provide cluster-valid calibration/inference, uncertainty on coverage, and prespecified conditional/worst-group diagnostics; revise all conformal and “for this situation” language accordingly.
2. **Perform a full selective-prediction and shift audit.** Report acceptance, abstention reasons, interval width and error jointly for every source and benchmark arm, including all-candidate denominators and risk–coverage curves; do not claim transfer from alarm-rate non-inflation alone.
3. **Replace the human–AV moment ratio with a crossed scenario-by-driver/system analysis.** Give run-level data flow, missing-cell accounting, uncertainty for Fig. 6a/c, and direct heterogeneity/equivalence tests for Fig. 6b.
4. **Redesign the consequence analysis to reduce phase and kinematic confounding.** Use non-overlapping landmark or first-alert risk sets, prespecified history adjustment, balance/overlap diagnostics, pre-trend and negative-control checks, and report both permutation tests.
5. **Establish a multiplicity and safety-claim hierarchy.** Select a primary consequence endpoint, use simultaneous or adjusted inference for the rest, and use prespecified non-inferiority margins before stating that emergency risk does not rise.
6. **Evaluate the sequential operating point or narrow the contribution.** Either freeze and validate episode-level alert logic under dependence or describe the method consistently as a frame-level atypicality diagnostic.
7. **Validate robustness and construct meaning.** Add IPV-model sensitivity analyses and, for the social-compliance claim, independent human-judgement or controlled-behaviour validation; otherwise narrow the title and interpretation to model-estimated atypicality.
