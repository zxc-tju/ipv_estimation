# Reviewer C

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes an online, abstaining monitor that compares an inverse-planning estimate of interaction preference value (IPV) with a situation-dependent human reference interval. It combines naturalistic trajectory data, split-conformal calibration, a matched-scenario automated-vehicle benchmark and a human-driver arm. The paper is commendably explicit that atypicality is neither inappropriateness nor harm. However, its main statistical claims are not yet supported at the level required for *Nature Machine Intelligence*. The conformal estimand and exchangeability unit are unclear; pointwise marginal coverage is repeatedly described in language suggestive of conditional and shifted-population validity; abstention can hide failures under shift; and no sequential false-alarm guarantee is provided for a framewise monitor. The consequence analyses remain highly selected observational comparisons with possible temporal overlap, crossed clustering, extensive multiplicity and no non-inferiority analysis capable of supporting “without added emergencies.” The human arm lacks uncertainty for its headline comparisons and does not establish either calibration transfer or equivalence of consequence signatures. Finally, the IPV is a model-derived measurement without sufficient validation of measurement invariance between humans and automated systems. The framework may become valuable, but the present manuscript requires a major statistical redesign and substantial tempering of claims.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Methods 4.4; Algorithm 1; Fig. 4] — The conformal estimand, conformity unit and guarantee are not coherently defined.** The stated primary estimand is “case-balanced pointwise marginal coverage” at four progress anchors, with at most one calibration score per case per anchor. It is unclear whether the four anchor scores are pooled. If so, a case contributes up to four dependent scores, violating the ordinary exchangeability argument for the quoted split-conformal order statistic. It is also unclear how calibration at four anchors yields the claimed performance over 461,937 densely repeated moments in Fig. 4. That number is an empirical test-set moment count, not the effective sample size governing a finite-sample conformal guarantee. The manuscript also does not report calibration-set sizes, the number accepted after each gate, or the achieved quantile index. These omissions prevent verification of the central coverage claim. The authors should define one sampling unit and target distribution mathematically; keep all windows/agents from a case and all cases from a scene in one split; report split and calibration counts by source and anchor; and use either one randomly selected anchor per case, a cluster-conformal construction, or a case-level vector score (for example, a maximum over prespecified anchors) with a guarantee matching the desired pointwise or simultaneous estimand. Coverage uncertainty must be computed at the independent case/scene level rather than from the frame count.

2. **[Abstract; Results 2.3 and 2.5; Methods 4.3–4.4; Figs. 4 and 6] — Marginal calibration is conflated with conditional validity and transfer under distribution shift.** Conformalized quantile regression can produce feature-adaptive intervals, but ordinary split conformal calibration guarantees marginal coverage over exchangeable accepted test units; it does not guarantee coverage conditional on the situation, source, risk group or time, and it provides no guarantee after the move from naturalistic open-road data to a different country, closed course and staged conflicts. The manuscript acknowledges this boundary in places, yet elsewhere calls the interval a “calibrated situation-conditioned human reference range” and interprets non-inflated alarms as evidence that the reference “survives the move.” The human course rate at the 90% level is 713/15,102 = 4.72%, far below both the nominal 10% and the naturalistic 9.72%. That may be conservative coverage, distributional mismatch, changed weighting, selective abstention or overly wide intervals; it is not evidence of calibration transfer by itself. The problem is particularly acute because the monitor speaks on only about one benchmark interaction moment in five, whereas naturalistic support abstention is reported as 5.08%. Coverage among accepted points can look favorable precisely because difficult shifted points are rejected. Required remedies are source-, scenario-, driver- and state-stratified coverage/width/abstention results with cluster confidence intervals; an explicit coverage–abstention trade-off; reporting over all active interactions as well as accepted ones; a prespecified non-inferiority/equivalence margin for alarm inflation; and substantially narrower language about external validity. If a formal shifted-population guarantee is claimed, an appropriate weighted or shift-aware method and its assumptions must actually be implemented and tested.

3. **[Results 2.4–2.5; Methods 4.4; Figs. 5–6] — A pointwise 90% monitor is evaluated as though it were a sequential alarm system, without sequential error control.** Repeated framewise testing almost guarantees at least one excursion during a sufficiently long interaction, even when every individual frame has nominal 90% marginal coverage; temporal dependence then produces clusters of flags. This makes scenario-level statements such as “speaks at least once,” counts of runs with any flagged moment, and persistence-based planner warnings uninterpretable without an episode-level false-alarm estimand. The manuscript says sequential warnings were tuned on an independent guard set, but provides neither the rule nor test-set operating characteristics. The trajectory-wise simultaneous variant is mentioned only as a boundary. For an online monitor, this is not secondary. The authors should prespecify whether the deployment target is pointwise coverage, probability of any false flag per interaction, false flag-cluster rate, expected run length, or a persistent-warning error rate; calibrate the corresponding trajectory/episode statistic on independent data; and report both pointwise and sequence-level sensitivity, specificity, abstention and delay on the untouched test set. Claims about runtime monitoring and planner-facing warnings should be restricted to whichever level is actually validated.

4. **[Methods 4.1–4.4; Results 2.1–2.3] — The predictive pipeline is insufficiently specified to assess leakage, reproducibility or nominal coverage.** Essential details are absent: the quantile-regression model family and objective; all situation features and their timestamps; preprocessing; hyperparameter search and selection criterion; exact train/guard/calibration/test sizes by source; random seeds; support and out-of-distribution algorithms; thresholds and how they were tuned; missing-data handling; and whether both agents, overlapping windows, repeated traversals and related map locations remain in the same split. “Frozen” and “held sealed” are assertions rather than an auditable protocol. Fig. 2 is explicitly based on the development split, but no corresponding confirmatory result is presented. The mention of a separate claims register cannot substitute for information in the paper. Moreover, “causal risk proxies” are not defined, and Fig. 4 says no observed risk measure is used online; the reader cannot determine whether any feature uses future trajectory information or an outcome-adjacent transformation. A complete model/split specification, participant-to-window flow diagram, leakage audit and untouched-test analysis are needed. Results developed on the development set must be labelled exploratory unless independently replicated.

5. **[Results 2.4 and 2.6; Methods 4.5; Fig. 5 and Fig. ED2] — The consequence analysis lacks a defensible observational estimand and is vulnerable to reverse causation and temporal leakage.** Flagged and within-range moments are not exchangeable treatments. A flag is a deterministic function of recent trajectory, interaction state and model fit, all of which also predict subsequent margins and counterpart responses. Matched scenarios do not remove moment-level confounding. The estimator uses a one-second rolling window; Fig. ED2 visually suggests that the counterpart’s speed fall is already under way near the selected flag onset; and “speed range over the window” may overlap the flag-defining interval. The manuscript states that analyses control for scenario and initial risk, but Fig. 5 presents raw distributions and ratios and gives no adjusted model, covariate set or adjusted estimate. The temporal estimand must be defined at a landmark that excludes all data used to form the flag, preferably using the first eligible persistent flag per run. Pre-alert trends and lead/lag placebo analyses are needed. Comparisons should be made within scenario and risk strata, with explicit adjustment for pre-alert kinematics, time in interaction, support/readability and system/driver. If the authors retain descriptive analyses only, terms such as “consequence,” “followed by” and “compression caused by atypicality” must be replaced by language that cannot be read causally.

6. **[Fig. 5; Results 2.4 and 2.6] — “Without added emergencies” is not established.** Failure to find an increase is not evidence of safety equivalence. For the ego-margin threshold below 3 s, the reported 95% interval is −3.14 to +3.15 percentage points, which permits a material increase. Negative point estimates at other thresholds are also confounded observational comparisons, not causal safety bounds. The four nested ego thresholds, three braking thresholds and several ordinary-interaction outcomes create a multiple-comparison family, yet the figure uses unadjusted 95% intervals and asterisks. The authors should define one or a small number of primary emergency endpoints, specify a substantively justified non-inferiority margin, use cluster-aware one-sided simultaneous intervals, and report absolute risks and adjusted risk differences. Until the upper bounds exclude those margins, the correct claim is that no increase was detected under the present descriptive comparison, not that emergencies stayed at or below the within-range rate.

7. **[Figs. 3, 5 and 6; Methods 4.5–4.6] — The inferential unit and dependence structure are mishandled or undocumented.** The reported sample sizes are mostly moments, although moments are nested within interaction windows, runs, systems/drivers and scenarios. Bootstrapping 175 scenarios for Fig. 5 may address scenario dependence, but it does not automatically address the crossed dependence induced when multiple automated systems encounter the same scenarios or when systems contribute multiple runs. The human arm is explicitly a 20-driver × 15-scenario crossed design, for which neither a moment-level analysis nor a one-way case bootstrap is sufficient. The headline 2.1-fold rate contrast and 15/15 scenario ordering are shown without uncertainty. The authors must define the population estimand (equal weight per moment, interaction, run, system/driver or scenario), justify that choice, and use a crossed multilevel model, multiway cluster-robust inference, or a two-way bootstrap that reflects both participant/system and scenario sampling. Report effective independent sample sizes, intraclass correlations or design effects, and confidence intervals for every headline contrast. Median ratios should be accompanied by absolute effects because their stability depends on the denominator.

8. **[Results 2.5; Methods 4.6; Fig. 6] — The human arm does not support the claimed calibration and “shared consequence signature.”** Fig. 6 explicitly omits uncertainty for key flag-rate and scenario-paired results. Calling the naturalistic and closed-course drivers “the population that defined the reference” also obscures a major shift: they are different samples, countries, apparatuses and tasks. A lower flag rate is only a one-sided observation, not evidence that the same reference distribution holds. Likewise, qualitative alignment of marker directions or separate within-group significance cannot establish that the human and automated-vehicle consequence signatures are the same. This requires direct driver-type × flag-status interaction tests or prespecified equivalence tests for each primary endpoint, with multiplicity control and crossed driver/scenario inference. Flag rates must be standardized to a common scenario distribution and accompanied by intervals; sensitivity to equal driver, system, scenario and moment weighting should be shown. Participant recruitment, exclusions, missing cells and any repeated runs also need reporting.

9. **[Eq. 1; Methods 4.1–4.3; Results throughout] — The reference target is a model-derived score whose validity and measurement invariance are not established.** Split conformal can calibrate the distribution of the estimated IPV, but it cannot validate IPV as a latent social preference. The manuscript acknowledges this once, yet subsequently speaks of “human preference” and interprets automated vehicles’ higher flag rate as social atypicality. The real-interaction controls in Fig. 2 demonstrate greater discriminability, not construct validity. The reliability cutoff (largest candidate weight above 0.20 versus a uniform value of about 0.143), seven-point grid, fixed likelihood scale and map-centreline reference could induce source- and agent-type-dependent measurement error. In particular, automated trajectories may fit the candidate generator differently from human trajectories, producing apparent flag inflation even under identical social conduct. The authors need residual and posterior-weight diagnostics by source and agent type; sensitivity to grid, likelihood scale, window length, reference geometry and gating thresholds; synthetic or controlled experiments with known preferences; and an explicit measurement-invariance analysis. Unless such evidence is supplied, all reference and flag claims should be phrased as properties of this IPV estimator, not of social preference itself.

10. **[Results 2.2–2.3; Figs. 3–4] — Several substantive interpretations do not follow from the displayed analyses.** First, failure of counterpart IPV or ego history to improve a prediction metric does not show that observable situation “suffices” or that partner correlation is “already carried” by the situation; noisy inputs, insufficient power, model misspecification and collinearity are alternatives. Report cross-validated incremental performance with confidence intervals and a prespecified equivalence margin, not a null-result narrative. Second, the unexplained 79.1% of variance cannot be attributed to “the interaction itself” or declared unrecoverable: it also contains measurement error, omitted variables and model error. Third, leave-one-source-out prediction in Fig. 3c gives zero or negative \(R^2\) in two sources, directly challenging transport of the conditional surface. The main validation should therefore include leave-one-source-out coverage, interval width, abstention and score—not merely pooled internal coverage. Fourth, the text claims a width reduction “above 40%,” whereas Fig. 4 shows reductions of 21%, 20% and 8% relative to the admissible range (and a 95% interval still spanning 92% of that range). The estimand behind the 40% statement must be reconciled with the figure, and “narrow” should be justified by operational utility rather than relative wording.

11. **[Results 2.2–2.5; Figs. 3–6] — Denominators, exclusions and analysis populations are not traceable.** The risk-bin counts in Fig. 3a total 37,495 rather than the 38,228 cases described; source counts differ between text and Fig. 3b; Fig. 4 uses 461,937 moments for interval evaluation and 486,660 for variance explained; Fig. 5 analyzes 472 assertive atypical and 11,669 within-range moments, whereas Fig. 6 reports 14,099 judgeable automated-vehicle moments and a 9.845% overall outside rate. These differences may have legitimate explanations (missing risk, accommodating-side deviations, unavailable consequences or different gates), but they materially affect each estimand. Provide a single flow diagram and table giving, for each data source and analysis, total scenes/cases/runs/moments, exclusions by reason, active/readable/supported counts, both atypical sides, outcome availability, and the independent resampling unit. Percentages should always state numerator, denominator and weighting rule.

## 3. Minor issues

1. Report uncertainty for achieved coverage in Fig. 4; the apparent precision of differences such as +0.03 percentage points is misleading when the effective unit is a case or scene rather than a frame.
2. Give the confidence interval for the middle-risk estimate in Fig. 3a and a formal interaction test for the claimed sign reversal across risk strata.
3. Define every “margin,” consequence window, emergency threshold and braking endpoint, including units, direction, censoring and the rationale for each threshold.
4. Replace “causal risk proxies” with “prospectively available risk proxies” unless a causal estimand and identification argument are supplied.
5. Report absolute counterpart-response changes in addition to ratios of medians.
6. Explain whether 80%, 90% and 95% intervals come from separately trained quantile models and separately calibrated radii, and how quantile crossing/rearrangement affects coverage.
7. Specify whether the 5.08% abstention in Fig. 4 includes all gate reasons or only low human support; elsewhere the reasons and denominators appear different.
8. The 95% context-conditioned interval spans 92% of the admissible IPV range, so its practical resolution should be discussed explicitly.
9. Reference 53 still contains an instruction to verify the challenge edition/year; this must be resolved before publication.
10. Correct “an readability indicator” in Results 2.1 and “the the onset” in Methods 4.5.
11. State ethics approval, consent, compensation and safety oversight for the 20-driver real-vehicle experiment.
12. The title and repeated phrase “social compliance” remain stronger than the validated endpoint. A title centered on online monitoring of conditional social atypicality would better match the evidence unless external normative validity is added.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What is the exact exchangeable unit for conformal calibration: scene, interaction case, agent-case, case-anchor or frame? How many independent units enter each split, source, anchor and conformal quantile?
2. Are the four anchor scores pooled when computing \(c_\alpha\)? If yes, what justifies treating within-case scores as exchangeable observations; if no, how are anchor-specific radii applied between anchors?
3. Which coverage statement is formally guaranteed—accepted-point marginal, case-balanced anchor-level, source-conditional or trajectory-simultaneous—and which Fig. 4 number estimates that same estimand?
4. What are coverage, interval width and abstention by source, risk band, geometry, progress anchor and support score? What happens in leave-one-source-out reference fitting?
5. In the automated benchmark and human arm, what fraction of all active interaction moments is lost at each gate, by scenario and driver/system? Are flag rates computed only after support selection?
6. What exactly is the independently calibrated persistence rule, and what is its test-set per-interaction false-warning probability and detection delay?
7. Which trajectory samples used to construct the flag can also enter each “post-verdict” outcome? Can the authors provide a strict non-overlapping landmark analysis and pre-trend/lead placebo plots?
8. What adjusted model underlies the statement that consequences were controlled for scenario and initial risk? Where are the adjusted estimates, and how were system/driver and scenario clustering handled?
9. What non-inferiority margin would count as “no added emergencies,” and do simultaneous upper confidence bounds exclude it for every claimed endpoint?
10. How were the 15 human-arm scenarios selected from the larger benchmark, and how are drivers and automated systems weighted when computing the 2.1-fold contrast? What is its crossed-design confidence interval?
11. What direct test supports equivalence of the human and automated-vehicle consequence signatures, rather than merely similar directions?
12. How do the authors exclude differential IPV model fit between human and automated trajectories as the explanation for the higher automated-vehicle flag rate?
13. What prediction metric, uncertainty interval and equivalence margin support the claim that counterpart IPV and ego history add no measurable information?
14. How is the stated “above 40%” width reduction calculated, given the 21%, 20% and 8% reductions shown in Fig. 4?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Reformulate and re-run the calibration analysis around a clearly defined independent unit and deployment estimand.** Provide a valid case/scene-level conformal construction, split audit, calibration counts, cluster uncertainty and an independently tested trajectory-level warning guarantee.
2. **Perform a full distribution-shift and selective-prediction audit.** Report source/scenario/group coverage, interval width, support and all abstention reasons together; include leave-one-source-out evaluation and sharply limit claims for the shifted closed-course population.
3. **Redesign the consequence analysis as a prespecified landmarked observational study.** Use first eligible alerts, non-overlapping future outcomes, pre-trend/placebo checks, explicit adjustment, and crossed scenario × system/driver inference. Remove causal language that remains unsupported.
4. **Replace “without added emergencies” with a formal non-inferiority analysis** using prespecified safety margins and simultaneous cluster-aware confidence bounds, or withdraw that conclusion.
5. **Re-analyze the human arm with uncertainty and direct comparisons.** Standardize weighting, use crossed-design inference, give intervals for flag-rate ratios and scenario differences, and test driver-type × flag interactions or equivalence for the claimed shared signature.
6. **Establish measurement robustness and invariance of IPV.** Add agent-type/source diagnostics and sensitivity analyses; clarify that conformal validity applies to the estimator output, not a latent preference, unless independent construct validation is supplied.
7. **Provide complete reproducible methods and a participant/data flow table.** Specify the quantile model, features and timing, tuning, gates, splits, counts, exclusions, software and randomness; reconcile every denominator and the interval-width discrepancy.
8. **Temper the central claims and title** so that marginal accepted-point calibration, observational associations and conditional atypicality are not presented as conditional coverage guarantees, harm bounds or validated social compliance.

## 6. Acceptance probability

- **As submitted:** 5%
- **Assuming a competent major revision:** 40%

The latter probability assumes that the authors can reconstruct the calibration and sequential-monitoring estimands without leakage, obtain materially similar results under cluster-appropriate and shift-aware analyses, and weaken claims that cannot be upgraded by the available data.
