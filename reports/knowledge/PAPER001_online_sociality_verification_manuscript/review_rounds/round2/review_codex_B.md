# Reviewer B report

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes a selective online monitor that estimates an interaction preference value (IPV), abstains when the estimate or human support is inadequate, and flags values outside a situation-conditioned human reference interval. It combines inverse planning, conformalized quantile regression, naturalistic data, a matched-scenario automated-vehicle benchmark, and a matched human arm. The paper currently delivers a retrospective, pointwise detector of conditional atypicality, not a validated runtime-assurance mechanism. Three issues are decisive: the nominal guarantee is marginal over highly dependent accepted frames and fails badly under some leave-one-source-out shifts; the actual sequential warning rule, event-level false-alarm behaviour, computational latency and safety-interface contract are not evaluated; and the consequence analyses do not establish value beyond the kinematic variables already used by the monitor. Moreover, the reference calibrates the distribution of IPV estimator outputs, not estimation error relative to a validated social-preference construct. The abstention cascade also excludes most benchmark candidate moments without a risk-stratified coverage account. The idea is potentially consequential, but the present claims exceed the evidence. A major revision must establish cluster/trajectory-level calibration, sequential operating characteristics, estimator validity and uncertainty, baseline-adjusted consequence evidence, and a strict distinction between human atypicality, social compliance and safety assurance.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Sections 2.1, 4.2 and 4.4; Algorithm 2] — The paper evaluates a pointwise test but claims an online monitor.** Verdicts are produced at 10 Hz, yet the planner-facing object is the unspecified “separately-calibrated persistence” operation in Algorithm 2, line 15. A 90% pointwise interval naturally places about 10% of in-distribution human moments outside the interval. Repeated testing across a trajectory can therefore make the probability of at least one warning, warning duration and alert chattering radically different from the pointwise rate. No temporal semantics, hysteresis/dwell rule, episode-level false-alarm rate, time-to-detection, warning duration, or simultaneous/time-uniform guarantee is reported. This is the central distinction between a statistical score and a runtime monitor. At NMI level, the actual emitted warning must be the evaluated object. The authors should freeze and specify the temporal warning rule; report per-interaction and per-driving-hour false alarms, first-alert timing, detection delay, alert duration and chattering; and provide trajectory-wise or time-uniform calibration appropriate to that rule. If this cannot be done, all planner-facing and runtime-monitor claims should be reduced to “pointwise retrospective screening.”

2. **[Section 4.4; Fig. 4] — The stated conformal guarantee is too weak for the claim and is not justified for the sampling structure.** The conformal quantile is formed from 455,723 accepted moments, although moments within an interaction and scene are strongly dependent. Splitting whole scenes prevents leakage, but it does not make hundreds of thousands of moment scores exchangeable independent calibration units, nor does it justify counting long interactions more heavily. The manuscript acknowledges only pointwise marginal coverage over accepted moments, which is not a per-interaction, state-conditional or sequential guarantee. More seriously, leave-one-source-out 90% coverage falls to 0.743 for Waymo and 0.750 for Lyft; Argoverse-2 reaches 0.900 only with 44.3% abstention. These are not minor deviations around nominal coverage but failures under precisely the heterogeneity relevant to deployment. A fixed pre-trained selection gate can be compatible with conformal inference, but the exchangeability target and the distribution conditional on acceptance must be stated and defended. The remedy is cluster- or trajectory-level conformal calibration with scenes/interactions as exchangeable units, alongside group/state-conditional and simultaneous coverage, confidence intervals over clusters, and coverage–abstention curves under source and scenario shift. Alternatively, the operational design domain must be explicitly restricted to the mixture on which the pointwise guarantee is supported.

3. **[Sections 2.1 and 4.1–4.3; Eqs. 1–3] — The monitored quantity and its uncertainty are insufficiently specified and partly self-referential.** The human reference target is the “human current IPV” produced by the same inverse-planning estimator used on the monitored vehicle. Thus conformal coverage describes the distribution of estimator outputs under the reference population; it does not calibrate error relative to a latent preference, social judgement or behavioural ground truth. Equation 1 omits the forward objective and constraints that generate candidate trajectories, the definitions of individual and group cost, solver details, and the dependence assumptions required to interpret negative IPV as assertiveness. The reported “reliability” is only concentration over seven model-generated candidates. A largest normalized weight just above 0.20, compared with 1/7 under uniformity, is not shown to predict estimator error or identification. The close agreement with an offline version sharing the same model is also not independent validation. The authors should fully specify the forward model and candidate generator, validate IPV against an external construct or carefully rename it as a model-derived behavioural index, calibrate the concentration statistic against reproducibility or error, propagate IPV uncertainty into the interval-membership decision, and report sensitivity to the candidate grid, likelihood scale, window length, reference geometry and model mismatch.

4. **[Sections 2.3–2.4 and 4.4–4.5; Fig. 4] — Selective coverage and abstention are not reported end to end.** The prominently stated 5.08% abstention is only support abstention among 486,660 already eligible human test anchors. Elsewhere, 3,202,646 of 4,497,368 natural-corpus anchor rows are readable, whereas only 14,099 of 67,861 benchmark candidate moments receive a verdict (20.78%). Those are very different denominators and operating regimes. The paper does not provide a single cascade from all sensed frames through active interaction, stable counterpart/map/role, solver success, estimator readability, human support, pointwise verdict and sequential warning. Nor does it show whether abstention concentrates in high-risk, unusual, fast-changing or otherwise important interactions. In runtime assurance, a monitor that is calibrated only where it speaks can still be unsafe or operationally unhelpful if silence is selective. Provide the full numerator/denominator cascade, reason codes and duration distributions for every source and for human versus automated runs; report per-run and risk-stratified coverage, longest abstention stretches and the fraction of interactions never judgeable; and evaluate whether Low-Human-Support or Out-of-Distribution abstention is enriched in consequential cases.

5. **[Sections 2.4, 4.5; Fig. 5 and Fig. ED2] — The consequence evidence does not establish predictive or incremental information supplied by IPV.** Each verdict uses the one-second trajectory window ending at time (t), plus relative kinematics and online risk proxies; the “post-verdict” outcome window begins at (t). Minimum time-to-collision and counterpart speed change can therefore be continuations of interaction compression already present in the inputs. Many overlapping flagged moments from the same stretch are treated as exposures. Resampling scenario runs handles some clustering, but it does not create temporal separation, remove state confounding or show that IPV adds information beyond simple kinematic monitors. The whole-sequence timing placebo does not answer that question. The lower emergency rates among flagged moments further show that this is not a safety-risk predictor and may partly identify low-risk negotiation regimes. The authors should analyse first alert/onset per interaction with a non-overlapping prediction horizon and meaningful lead time; compare against relative kinematics, TTC/PET proxies, braking, trajectory residual/surprise and a learned anomaly baseline; test the incremental contribution of IPV beyond (z_t); and use paired/hierarchical models within scenario and risk strata. Claims should remain associational unless a design supporting causal interpretation is added.

6. **[Sections 2.5 and 4.6; Fig. 6] — The matched human arm does not yet validate transfer or the reported automated-versus-human contrast.** Twenty drivers each face fifteen scenarios, creating crossed repeated measures by driver and scenario, but the main comparisons use 15,102 human moments and 14,099 automated moments and explicitly omit uncertainty for key rates. The expected 300 human driver–scenario runs, missing runs and exclusion flow are not reported. A 4.7% outside rate relative to the natural-driving 9.72% rate shows no alarm inflation under this selected accepted-moment measure; it does not establish that the instrument “survives the move,” because conservative intervals, selective abstention, changed state mix or apparatus effects can all lower the rate. “15/15 scenarios above parity” also requires scenario-paired uncertainty and must account for the systems/drivers sampled within each scenario. Reanalyse with crossed driver/scenario/system effects or cluster randomization/permutation, provide confidence intervals for all rates and ratios, show judgeability and abstention for each arm, disclose the complete run flow, and limit the transfer conclusion to the quantities actually tested.

7. **[Introduction; Sections 2.6, 3 and 4.6, “Planner interface”] — A diagnostic atypicality score is conflated with runtime assurance.** No end-to-end execution on vehicle hardware is evaluated, and no worst-case execution time, sensor-to-verdict latency, deadline-miss behaviour, numerical-failure policy or fault-containment argument is provided. More importantly, the suggested actions—soft cost, warning, fallback under sustained deviation, and degraded mode on abstention—are not implemented or verified. A socially motivated fallback can conflict with collision avoidance, RSS-like responsibility constraints, reachable safe sets or another hard-safety supervisor. A runtime-assurance claim requires an explicit architecture and contracts: which component has authority, what assumptions the social monitor makes, how stale/uncertain inputs are handled, how a social action is prevented from violating the physical safety envelope, and what safe action follows abstention or failure. The authors should either present and evaluate such an architecture, including timing measurements and non-interference with a higher-priority safety monitor, or consistently characterize the contribution as an offline-validated advisory monitor whose planner interface remains future work.

8. **[Title, Abstract, Introduction and Discussion] — Human typicality is not social compliance, despite the manuscript’s caveats.** The paper repeatedly acknowledges that atypicality is not inappropriateness, yet its title, “Competitive-Deviation” label, “responsible social monitor” language and proposed fallback actions restore a normative interpretation. Natural human driving is heterogeneous, culturally and geographically contingent, and can include unsafe, discourteous or strategically undesirable behaviour. A central 90% reference interval also makes minority but acceptable styles atypical by construction. No human preference, acceptability, norm-violation or harm label validates “compliance.” This is not a semantic detail: it determines whether the system is an empirical deviation detector or an assurance mechanism. Either retitle and consistently reframe the paper around online monitoring of situation-conditioned behavioural atypicality, or add independent normative validation with human judgements/outcomes, subgroup analyses and evidence that actions based on the flag improve interaction quality without degrading safety or unfairly penalizing legitimate behavioural variation.

## 3. Minor issues

1. The opening statement that AVs “are certified for collision safety” is not generally true. ISO 21448 and UL 4600 do not provide a universal guarantee that a deployed AV is collision-safe.
2. The statement that formal online verification can “guarantee legal safety” conflates formal satisfaction of a chosen model/specification with legality and real-world safety under modelling and perception uncertainty.
3. Section 4.1 declares θ in \([-π/2,π/2]\), but the seven evaluated candidates cover only \([-3π/8,3π/8]\). Clarify the unrepresented endpoints and possible boundary bias.
4. Define (u_a(t)) mathematically. Equation 2 uses (u_a(t)<u_0), while the operational rule is expressed using a maximum normalized weight; the direction and transformation should be explicit.
5. Equation 1 and the likelihood notation need a complete definition of conditioning variables, horizon alignment and normalization. The current notation does not allow reproduction from the paper.
6. “Counterpart-Unreadable” in Algorithm 2 appears to be emitted when counterpart-identity stability (c_i) fails; distinguish identity uncertainty from an unreadable counterpart preference.
7. Figure 2a should name and define the plotted concentration-change statistic and its units directly, rather than only interpreting negative values as sharper identification.
8. Figure 3a uses realized PET for the descriptive event-level result, whereas the online monitor excludes realized PET. Mark this offline/online distinction more prominently to avoid anticipatory interpretation.
9. Figure 4c needs the exact out-of-fold model, grouping/split unit and uncertainty for the reported 20.9% explained variance.
10. The manuscript should reconcile 38,228 interaction cases in Section 2.2 with the 26,828 cases entering the conformal split and state each exclusion explicitly.
11. The flow from 519 assertive-side judgeable moments to 472 ego-margin moments and 469 counterpart records should be tabulated, including whether missingness depends on the verdict or outcome.
12. The frozen battery of correlated thresholds in Fig. 5 avoids threshold cherry-picking but not multiplicity. Provide simultaneous intervals or clearly label all intervals as endpoint-wise descriptive intervals.
13. Replace “without adding emergencies” and “emergency margins stay at or below” with explicitly associational wording; absence of a higher observed rate is not proof that the monitor cannot add emergencies.
14. Report exact rates rather than “about one in five” and “nearly nine in ten” at first mention: 14,099/67,861 = 20.78% of candidate moments and 231/267 = 86.52% of runs.
15. Explain how 15 scenario templates were selected and what population of scenarios they are intended to represent. Fifteen repeated templates do not support broad scenario-level generalization without a defined sampling frame.
16. Figure ED2 is one of only two runs satisfying stringent display criteria. Retain it only as an explicitly exceptional diagnostic example and provide a systematic distribution of alert-stretch examples or onset-aligned trajectories.
17. Figure ED1 contains the title wording “changes width with with the situation.”
18. “No engineering failures” in the benchmark should be defined and distinguished from the 1,826 solver failures reported for the natural corpus.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What is the formal exchangeability unit for the conformal guarantee: moment, interaction, scene, driver or source? What result justifies using all accepted moments as calibration scores despite within-scene dependence?
2. What exactly is the persistence rule in Algorithm 2, line 15? On what independent data was it tuned, and what are its per-interaction false-alarm probability, expected warning duration and detection delay?
3. What are the complete attrition counts from all sensor frames/candidate moments to active interactions, readable IPV estimates, supported reference queries, pointwise verdicts and emitted warnings in each dataset and each human/automated arm?
4. Does the maximum candidate weight predict estimator error, repeatability or downstream stability? Why is 0.20 an adequate identification threshold for seven candidates?
5. Does IPV or distance beyond the reference interval predict any consequence after controlling for every component of (z_t), current TTC/relative speed, scenario identity and the raw trajectory residual?
6. How much genuine lead time exists between the first information used in a warning and the first outcome sample? Do the results remain under non-overlapping first-alert windows?
7. Of the planned (20\times15=300) human driver–scenario runs, how many were completed, judgeable and analysed, and what were the reasons for all omissions?
8. What are the per-driver and per-scenario distributions of human flag rates? Is the 2.1-fold automated-versus-human contrast robust to crossed driver/scenario/system inference rather than moment counts?
9. What hardware, software process and input pipeline would execute the monitor online? What are median, tail and worst-case latency, and what verdict is produced on deadline miss, solver failure or stale perception?
10. How is a social-monitor fallback prevented from violating a hard safety envelope or overriding a reachable-safe-set/RSS-style supervisor? What component has final authority?
11. Which geographical, demographic, driving-style or scenario subgroups have been examined for interval width, coverage and flag-rate disparities?
12. Which exact human judgement or outcome would falsify the interpretation that an assertive-side IPV deviation is socially undesirable rather than merely uncommon?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Define and evaluate the actual sequential monitor.** Freeze the persistence/alert semantics and report trajectory-level calibration, false alerts, warning duration, chattering and detection lead time.
2. **Replace moment-level conformal claims with sampling-appropriate guarantees.** Use scene/interaction-clustered or hierarchical/trajectory-wise conformal calibration, then quantify conditional coverage and abstention under source and scenario shift.
3. **Fully specify and validate the IPV measurement model.** Include the forward costs, constraints, candidate generation, solver and uncertainty propagation; validate or narrow the interpretation of “preference” and “reliability.”
4. **Provide complete end-to-end selective-monitor accounting.** Report every abstention reason and denominator by source, risk, scenario and human/automated arm, including run-level silence and abstention duration.
5. **Reanalyse consequences as prospective and incremental.** Use first-alert/non-overlapping windows and compare IPV flags with kinematic, TTC/risk, trajectory-surprise and learned-residual baselines under matched hierarchical inference.
6. **Reanalyse the human arm with crossed repeated-measures inference.** Report the full run/exclusion flow, uncertainty on every comparison, per-driver/per-scenario distributions and judgeability parity.
7. **Separate atypicality monitoring from normative and safety assurance claims.** Retitle/reframe unless independent preference/harm validation is added; remove unimplemented planner-action claims or supply a safety-prioritized architecture and non-interference/timing evaluation.
8. **Audit reproducibility and sensitivity.** Report frozen hyperparameters, grid/window/likelihood/support sensitivities, subgroup results, simultaneous uncertainty for threshold batteries, and exact source/scenario sampling boundaries.
