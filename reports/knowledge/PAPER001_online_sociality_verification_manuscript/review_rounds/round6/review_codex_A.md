# Referee report

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes an online monitor whose stated question is whether “the AV’s current interaction behaviour [is] within the human range for this situation” (Discussion), using an interaction preference value (IPV), an explicit abstention state and a situation-conditioned reference interval (Sections 2.1–2.3). It associates assertive-side flags with “compressed ordinary interaction” (Figure 5), while stating that “socially atypical is not socially inappropriate” (Introduction). The engineering synthesis is potentially useful, especially the explicit abstention state and the matched-human audit. However, the central interpretation is not established. IPV is validated mainly against its own inverse-planning construction and future kinematics, not against independent behavioural-science measures of social perception, preference, reciprocity, acceptability or burden. The monitor judges only “14,099 (20.8%)” of 67,861 benchmark candidate moments (Section 2.4), without showing that rejected moments are ignorable. Most importantly, the human-arm result indicates strong overcoverage after domain shift, while the reported AV rate “sits at the native level” (Section 2.5); this does not support the stated transfer or population comparison. The claimed independence from counterpart intention is also stronger than the ablation warrants. These are foundational issues requiring new validation rather than textual clarification.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **The measured construct has not been validated as “social,” so the title and compliance framing outrun the evidence.** [Location: Abstract; Introduction; Sections 2.1 and 4.6] — The manuscript defines IPV as “how an agent trades its own progress against the group’s” (Abstract) and more narrowly as “one continuous, online-computable dimension of social interaction” covering “how strongly the agent weights the counterpart’s cost against its own progress” (Section 2.1). This is a model-derived trade-off parameter, not by itself a validated measure of social understanding, acceptability, norm compliance, reciprocity or imposed burden. The manuscript commendably states that “socially atypical is not socially inappropriate” (Introduction), but still calls the system a monitor of “socially compliant autonomous driving” and labels one output `Competitive-Deviation`. The validation does not close this semantic gap: Methods 4.6 says that “harm labels and preference ratings were excluded as endpoints,” and the reported consequences are kinematic. At this journal’s level, a new social construct requires convergent and discriminant validity beyond correspondence with trajectories generated and interpreted through the same inverse-planning model. Remedy: validate IPV and its two-sided flags against independent human judgements and established behavioural constructs (for example perceived assertiveness/accommodation, predictability, acceptability, reciprocity and experienced burden), with blinded raters and inter-rater reliability; demonstrate discriminant validity against ordinary risk, progress and kinematic-surprise measures; and, absent such evidence, retitle and consistently reframe the contribution as monitoring *human-relative IPV atypicality*, not social compliance.

2. **The novelty claim is not demonstrated against the closest technical alternatives, and situation conditioning is presented as more conceptually distinctive than the evidence supports.** [Location: Introduction; Sections 2.3 and 4.3; Figure 4] — The Introduction claims that prior monitors and benchmarks do not “read[] a social preference, refer[] it to a calibrated situation-conditioned human range, or separate[] the moments at which a social judgement is meaningful.” Yet the primary comparison in Methods 4.3 is only among “a global reference, an offline oracle-risk ceiling, and the context-conditioned reference.” Context-conditioned prediction, learned behavioural surprise, inverse reward inference and selective prediction are established ingredients among the paper’s own cited literatures; combining them may be a useful system contribution, but no head-to-head result shows that IPV intervals detect something not already captured by a conditional trajectory-likelihood, expectation-violation or raw-kinematic monitor. Figure 4c reports that the situation explains only 20.9% of IPV variance, but this does not establish that the residual is socially meaningful or useful. Remedy: benchmark the proposed monitor against strong online baselines built from the same observable history and support policy, including conditional trajectory likelihood/surprise, future-motion prediction residuals, risk-conditioned kinematic envelopes and an unconditional IPV interval. Compare coverage–selectivity, alert timing, consequence association, human-rated social validity and incremental predictive value. The novelty claim should then be limited to what those comparisons demonstrate.

3. **The claimed independence from the counterpart’s inferred intention does not follow from the reported ablation.** [Location: Sections 2.1, 2.3, 3 and 4.3; Equation 1] — The paper concludes that monitoring “does not require inferring the counterpart’s hidden intention: the current observable situation suffices” (Section 2.3), because adding the counterpart’s current IPV yields a “paired 90% interval-score difference −0.0002, case-clustered p = 0.86” (Methods 4.3). This establishes, at most, no incremental interval-score benefit from one explicit counterpart-IPV input. It does not establish independence from counterpart intention or behaviour. Equation 1 conditions the ego estimate on the counterpart trajectory, and the situation vector includes counterpart velocity and heading, relative position and velocity, closing rate, heading difference, short-window histories and risk proxies (Methods 4.3). These are precisely the observable channels through which intention is expressed and can be statistically proxied. Remedy: replace the strong claim with the narrower statement that an additional explicit counterpart-IPV estimate did not improve interval score after observed interaction history was included. To establish a stronger result, recompute the ego estimator under controlled removal, perturbation and counterfactual substitution of counterpart trajectories; quantify alert stability and interval performance; and test whether counterpart-dependent features mediate the result.

4. **The human audit does not demonstrate calibration transfer, and the “2.1 times” human–AV contrast is not measurement-equivalent.** [Location: Sections 2.5 and 4.6; Figure 6] — Section 2.5 states that the natural-driving population falls outside the 90% interval on “9.72% of held-out judgeable moments,” whereas same-scenario human drivers are flagged on “4.7% of judgeable moments (713/15,102),” and concludes that “carrying the reference across country, apparatus and task does not inflate its alarms.” Lack of inflation is not calibration: 4.7% outside a nominal 90% interval indicates substantial overcoverage relative to the native 9.72% rate. The AV rate is “9.8%,” essentially the native rate, so the reported “2.1 times” contrast is generated by the unusually low matched-human rate rather than an elevated AV rate against the defining population. Moreover, Methods 4.5 says simulated counterparts reach an automated system “through its perception interface and a human driver through a head-mounted display,” and Figure 6 compares 20 drivers × 15 scenarios (300 runs) with 19 systems and 267 usable runs. Thus identical scenario labels and counterpart control do not ensure equivalent sensing, task experience, exposure, missingness or sampling units. A per-moment ratio also gives no population-level uncertainty that respects driver/system and temporal clustering; “15 of 15 scenarios” is not a substitute for such an analysis. Remedy: treat the 4.7% result as domain-shift overcoverage, not transfer calibration; report calibration and abstention by scenario and driver; use a hierarchical, scenario-matched analysis with driver/system/run as the sampling units, equalized scenario exposure, clustered uncertainty and explicit missing-run sensitivity; test arm-by-flag interactions for the claimed “same consequence signature”; and either establish apparatus equivalence experimentally or restrict the comparison to within-arm statements.

5. **Abstention is insufficiently validated as a principled reject option and can select away the most informative deployment states.** [Location: Sections 2.4, 4.4 and 4.5; Figures 4 and 6] — The monitor returns verdicts for “14,099 (20.8%)” of 67,861 benchmark candidate moments (Section 2.4). In the 36 always-silent runs, readability remains “53.78%” but the human-support pass rate is “0.07% (against 32.32% overall)” (Methods 4.5). The support gate is fixed at the “95th percentile of guard distances” and coverage is explicitly “pointwise marginal coverage over accepted moments” (Methods 4.4). These facts make the accepted set a highly selected population. Calibration conditional on acceptance says nothing about false reassurance or missed atypicality in the 79.2% of candidate moments for which no verdict is produced, and the 95th-percentile rule is not shown to optimize a behavioural or safety objective. Calling all rejected regions responsibly unknowable is not enough: the reject mechanism could systematically remove rare, high-conflict, novel or system-specific behaviour. Remedy: provide coverage–risk–abstention curves over gate thresholds; characterize rejection by scenario, system/driver, interaction phase, present risk and subsequent consequence; test whether flag and consequence estimates are robust under plausible support thresholds and weighting/censoring models; audit OOD detection against known shifts; and state an operational requirement for minimum scenario/run coverage. The monitor should not be described as deployable where it is silent for most candidate moments and entirely silent in 13.5% of runs without a defined fallback analysis.

6. **The consequence analysis does not isolate a response to the flag from persistence of the state that generated the flag.** [Location: Sections 2.4 and 4.5; Figure 5] — Methods 4.5 states that “outcome windows begin at the flagged moment” and that the verdict uses “the one-second window ending at that moment.” The outcome therefore begins with no washout after a state already containing the ego and counterpart kinematics used by both the IPV estimator and the conditional reference. The fixed three-second counterpart window can largely measure continuation of braking or conflict already underway; the open-ended ego window additionally varies in length with remaining run duration. The reported whole-trajectory timing placebo is suggestive (`p = 0.0199`), but the same Methods section reports that “a case-level label permutation on the same battery does not reach significance (p = 0.1493).” Repeated per-frame flags also create heavily overlapping outcome windows, so run-clustered resampling does not turn them into independent event onsets. Consequently, the phrase “followed by” and the interpretation of counterpart “response” are stronger than the design supports, even if no causal claim is made. Remedy: define independent alert onsets using the proposed persistence rule; exclude an explicit temporal buffer; show pre-trends and lead–lag effects; use fixed non-overlapping future horizons; match or weight flagged and within-range on the full current situation, pre-window kinematics, scenario and system; report run/episode-level effects; and explain the non-significant label-permutation result rather than privileging one placebo.

7. **Reliability and identifiability of IPV are not validated independently of the estimator’s own model.** [Location: Sections 2.1 and 4.1; Figure 2] — The “reliability” gate discards a reading when “the largest normalised weight falls below 0.20 (against 1/7 ≈ 0.14 for exactly uniform weights)” (Methods 4.1), using seven candidates, a one-second window and a Gaussian likelihood with `σ = 0.1` m. No recovery study establishes that 0.20 separates accurate from inaccurate preference estimates, that the coarse seven-point grid can identify the claimed continuous quantity, or that `σ = 0.1` m is robust to trajectory and map error. The reported correlation “≈ 0.993” is agreement with an offline estimate under the same map-lane reference, explicitly “not a causal-effect claim” (Methods 4.1), and Figure 2a tests weight concentration rather than parameter accuracy. Remedy: provide controlled recovery experiments with known preference parameters and realistic observation/map errors; sensitivity analyses over candidate grids, window length, likelihood scale, reference geometry and interaction model misspecification; calibration of the dispersion statistic against estimation error; and comparisons with alternative inverse-planning estimators. Until then, `reliability` should be called candidate-weight concentration, not evidence that the latent preference is correctly read.

## 3. Minor issues

1. [Location: Section 2.2; Figure 3] The headline “What holding priority means depends on the risk” is too interpretive for small descriptive contrasts formed using realised post-encroachment time. The caption itself says this is “an offline quantity used here for description only”; revise the heading to describe the observed IPV contrast without implying psychological meaning.
2. [Location: Sections 2.3 and 4.4; Figure 4] The manuscript reports source-wise 90% coverage of “88.2%, 95.9%, 85.9% and 88.2%” and leave-one-source-out coverage as low as “0.743” and “0.750.” These deviations should be visually prominent rather than relegated to Methods, because they qualify the claim of a calibrated reference.
3. [Location: Section 4.4] The paper uses `Low-Human-Support` and `Out-of-Distribution` as distinct reason codes, but the operational distinction is not sufficiently specified alongside the nearest-neighbour support rule. Define each trigger and its evaluation order unambiguously.
4. [Location: Sections 2.4 and 4.5; Figure 5] “Supported threshold” is easy to confuse with the human-support gate, despite the caption’s clarification. Use “interval-excluding-zero threshold” or another unambiguous term.
5. [Location: Methods opening; Planner interface] The manuscript defines online only as inputs being available at decision time and states that “end-to-end real-time operation on vehicle hardware is not evaluated.” Report computational latency, worst-case runtime, hardware and missed-deadline behaviour, or consistently use “causal/offline replay monitor” rather than implying demonstrated runtime readiness.
6. [Location: Figure ED2] The displayed run is selected from only “two meeting display criteria” and then ranked first. Although the caption discloses this, the figure is visually prominent and strongly confirmatory in appearance. Replace it with a prespecified random example or a compact set spanning typical and boundary cases.
7. [Location: Data Availability] The statement that human-arm records “are not released” prevents independent verification of a decisive comparison. Provide privacy-preserving per-run summaries, analysis-ready aggregates sufficient to reproduce all reported estimates, and executable analysis code under controlled access if raw trajectories cannot be shared.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What independent evidence shows that IPV, rather than merely its own/joint-cost parameterization, tracks social perception, reciprocity, acceptability or burden as understood in traffic psychology?
2. How much incremental information do IPV flags provide over a monitor using the same context vector, current TTC/closing rate, trajectory-prediction residual and behavioural surprise, under an identical abstention policy?
3. Does the claim that counterpart intention is unnecessary survive recomputing ego IPV without the counterpart trajectory, or under counterfactual substitution of counterpart histories?
4. Why should a 4.7% outside rate at nominal 90% coverage be interpreted as successful transfer rather than substantial overcoverage under domain shift?
5. What are the driver-clustered, system-clustered and scenario-matched uncertainty intervals for the 2.1× rate ratio, and how does the result change when each driver/system contributes equal weight?
6. Are rejected moments more likely to occur near conflict, during strong braking, in particular systems, or before adverse outcomes? What fraction of each scenario’s active interaction time receives a verdict?
7. Do consequence differences remain after defining non-overlapping alert onsets, imposing a post-verdict buffer, matching on all current-state inputs and testing pre-trends?
8. How sensitive are readings, gate pass rates and flags to the seven-point grid, `σ = 0.1` m, window length, map error and the 0.20 weight threshold?
9. What statistical test supports the assertion that humans and automated systems show the “same consequence signature,” rather than merely estimates in the same direction?
10. What inference is intended over the 19 automated systems: these particular challenge entries, a distribution of planners, or automated driving systems generally?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. Establish independent construct validity for IPV and both flag directions using blinded human and behavioural-science endpoints; otherwise remove “social compliance” as the claimed measured property.
2. Reanalyse the human audit as a measurement-equivalence and domain-shift study, with hierarchical scenario-matched inference, calibrated uncertainty, equalized sampling, missingness sensitivity and an explicit test of cross-arm consequence equivalence.
3. Validate abstention/selectivity across thresholds and characterize all rejected moments and silent runs with respect to scenario, system/driver, risk and subsequent outcomes.
4. Redesign the consequence analysis around independent alert onsets, non-overlapping fixed horizons, temporal buffers, pre-trends and matched current-state controls.
5. Add strong online kinematic, behavioural-surprise and trajectory-prediction baselines to show the incremental contribution of IPV and situation-conditioned reference intervals.
6. Narrow the counterpart-intention claim and test estimator dependence through removal and counterfactual perturbation of counterpart trajectories.
7. Validate IPV recovery and gate reliability under realistic model, observation and map errors, and report computational feasibility on target hardware.

## 6. Acceptance probability

- **As submitted:** 3%
- **Assuming a competent major revision:** 25%

## 7. Recommendation

**Reject**

The core idea may merit reconsideration after substantial new validation, but the present manuscript does not establish that its model-derived atypicality measure is a measure of social compliance, and its central transfer and human–AV comparison claims are not supported by a measurement-equivalent design.
