# Reviewer A report

## 1. Summary assessment

This manuscript proposes a selective, frame-level monitor that infers an interaction preference value (IPV) from recent trajectories, abstains when the estimate or human support is inadequate, and flags readings outside a situation-conditioned human reference interval. The paper combines four naturalistic datasets, split-conformal calibration, a matched-scenario real-vehicle benchmark, and a human-driver arm. It delivers a coherent engineering framework and explicit abstention logic. However, the central scientific interpretation is not established. IPV is inferred from the same trajectory/kinematic process that defines the context and strongly predicts subsequent margin and speed outcomes; no independent behavioural or perceptual criterion shows that the signal measures social preference rather than model mismatch or interaction phase. The pointwise conformal analysis treats densely repeated moments as calibration units, while severe leave-one-source-out undercoverage contradicts broad transfer claims. In the benchmark, overlapping frame-level exposures, weakly specified adjustment, and incomplete hierarchical inference prevent the consequence signature from validating the monitor. The human arm lacks essential participant, protocol, ethics and uncertainty reporting. Finally, a retrospectively replayable, non-anticipating computation is called a runtime monitor without latency or closed-loop evidence. The idea is potentially important, but the manuscript requires major new validation and methodological clarification before its NMI-level claims are supported.

## 2. Major weaknesses

1. **[Location: Abstract; Introduction; Sections 2.1, 2.4–2.6; Methods 4.1 and 4.5; Figs. 2, 5 and 6] — The social construct is not independently validated.**  
   The manuscript interprets the inferred angle as how an agent trades its own progress against the group's, and an assertive-side flag as weighting the counterpart's cost less than humans do. Yet the paper does not provide the candidate trajectory objective, the own-versus-group cost decomposition, or an independent test showing that variation in the reported IPV corresponds to perceived assertiveness, courtesy, accommodation, acceptability or burden. Agreement with an offline version of the same estimator is not construct validation. Nor is the reported “consequence signature” independent validation: IPV is inferred from the recent trajectories; the reference conditions on current relative kinematics and risk proxies; and the outcomes are post-verdict TTC/margin and speed changes that are mechanically autocorrelated with those trajectories and interaction phase. The monitor may therefore be detecting kinematic state or candidate-model residual rather than social preference. This matters at NMI level because the contribution is not generic anomaly detection: its claimed novelty and significance rest on a socially meaningful latent quantity. The authors should validate the signal against an independent criterion, preferably preregistered human judgements of held-out trajectory clips and controlled trajectory manipulations, and show incremental validity over simple kinematic, risk, surprise and model-residual baselines. At minimum, mediation/ablation analyses must establish that the consequence results are not reconstructed from variables already used to issue the flag.

2. **[Location: Sections 2.1 and 2.3; Methods 4.1–4.4; Algorithms 1–2] — The estimator and “readability” gate are insufficiently specified and not shown to measure estimation reliability.**  
   The seven candidate preferences, one-second window, Gaussian MSE likelihood and maximum-weight threshold are stated, but the candidate trajectory generator, dynamics, cost terms, optimisation, route assumptions, horizon, counterpart prediction and failure handling are not. The text parameterises IPV on \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\), whereas the frozen grid covers only \([-3\pi/8,3\pi/8]\); the “admissible range” used in Fig. 4 is consequently ambiguous. The threshold `max weight >= 0.20` is close to the uniform value of 1/7 and is justified only as discrimination among candidates, not as accuracy. Figure 2a tests concentration against control pairings, but there is no ground-truth or perturbation analysis showing that concentration predicts small IPV error; Fig. 2b in fact shows greater frame-to-frame movement in the “readable” subset. The very high correlation with an offline estimator is also uninformative if both share the same candidate model and reference geometry. For an online monitoring claim, the paper must provide a complete reproducible estimator specification and calibrate the reliability score against known or independently labelled quantities. Sensitivity is needed to the candidate grid, \(\sigma\), window length, map/route uncertainty, principal-counterpart selection, tracking noise and the 0.20 threshold. The complete gate accounting should be reported for every evaluation population, not only the naturalistic corpus.

3. **[Location: Section 2.3; Methods 4.3–4.4; Fig. 4] — The conformal guarantee and the claimed situation-conditioned calibration do not match the sampling structure.**  
   Calibration uses 455,723 accepted moments and testing uses 461,937 moments, although many moments come from the same interaction and scene. Splitting whole scenes prevents direct leakage, but standard split-conformal validity does not follow from treating all correlated within-scene moments as exchangeable calibration scores. Long interactions also receive more weight than short ones, so the estimand is a frame-weighted distribution rather than a driver-, interaction- or scene-level reference. The authors acknowledge dependence but then still describe the result as a conformal guarantee. Moreover, one global conformal radius establishes, at best, marginal coverage over accepted moments; conditioning a quantile model on state does not itself establish conditional coverage across risk, role, geometry, source or readability strata. This distinction is central because the paper's scientific claim is specifically *conditional* social atypicality. The analysis should be redone using a group-/block-aware conformal construction or a calibration sampling scheme whose exchangeability unit is the independent scene or interaction. Report uncertainty and coverage jointly with abstention by prespecified state groups, source, driver, scenario and interaction, including worst-group results. Also report per-interaction simultaneous error or false-alarm rates, because sequentially applying a 90% pointwise interval can produce a high probability of at least one alarm per episode even under a valid reference.

4. **[Location: Sections 2.2–2.5; Methods 4.4 and 4.6; Figs. 3, 4 and 6] — Distribution-shift results contradict the strongest transfer language, and the human-reference audit is not analysed as an equivalence/calibration study.**  
   Leave-one-source-out 90% coverage is only 0.743 for Waymo and 0.750 for Lyft; the Argoverse-2 fold reaches 0.900 only with 44.3% abstention. These are not minor deviations. They show that the reference can be badly miscalibrated on a held-out source even when the support gate accepts a state. The text correctly notes this boundary in Methods and Fig. 4, but elsewhere claims that the instrument “survives the move” and transfers across country, apparatus and task. The matched-course human flag rate of 4.7% is also far below both the nominal 10% outside rate and the 9.72% natural-driving test rate. Absence of alarm inflation is a one-sided statement, not evidence of calibration or transportability; overcoverage may make the monitor insensitive. The 9.72% natural test rate is largely a consequence of the calibration target and is not an independent apparatus-validity anchor. Further, the on-course drivers are not literally “the population that defined the reference”; they are a new sample under a different task and apparatus. Remedy requires source- and group-aware recalibration or a justified distribution-shift method, explicit equivalence/non-inferiority margins, confidence intervals for all rates, and a decomposition of shift in covariates, readability, support, interval width and conditional residuals. Claims should be limited to the tested populations unless the revised method achieves acceptable coverage under held-out-source evaluation.

5. **[Location: Sections 2.4–2.6; Methods 4.5; Figs. 5 and ED2] — The consequence analysis does not yet distinguish a flag effect from interaction phase, repeated exposure and scenario/system confounding.**  
   The unit being labelled is a moment, but adjacent flagged frames form stretches and their three-second outcome windows overlap. Thus 472 flagged moments within 120 runs are not 472 distinct interaction events; the estimand weights long flagged stretches more heavily. Bootstrapping 175 runs can improve interval estimation, but it does not correct the frame-weighted estimand or remove confounding. Flags may occur preferentially at a particular conflict phase, when current TTC is already falling and the counterpart has already begun to brake. “Comparisons within frozen situation cells” is too vague: the cells, overlap, balance diagnostics, model, effect aggregation and residual differences are not reported. The placebo reassignment is mentioned but no estimand or result is shown. Systems recur across scenarios and scenarios recur across systems, so run-only resampling also fails to represent the crossed system-by-scenario design. A defensible revision should collapse contiguous flags into prespecified episodes or select one index moment per interaction, present pre-verdict trajectories and event-time effects, enforce comparable time-at-risk, and use matched/weighted or hierarchical models with scenario and driving-system effects (and driver effects in the human arm). Show covariate balance, the placebo and negative-control leads in the main evidence. The language must remain associational unless a stronger design is supplied.

6. **[Location: Sections 2.4–2.6; Methods 4.5–4.6; Figs. 5 and 6] — “Interaction quality” and the claimed shared consequence signature are not adequately operationalised.**  
   A lower median ego margin and larger counterpart speed reduction are called a compression of ordinary interaction quality, but no independent evidence establishes that these changes are undesirable, effortful or socially dispreferred. The same flagged moments have *lower* emergency-margin and hard-braking rates, and the lower quartile of the margin is unchanged. This pattern is compatible with assertive but orderly negotiation, conservative test design, scenario phase selection, or scripted/reactive counterpart behaviour. It cannot currently support “interaction-benign,” burden or compliance language. The human arm is especially difficult to interpret because Section 4.6 describes scripted counterpart injections, whereas Section 4.5 excludes a “scripted-counterpart subset”; it is unclear which counterpart outcomes can actually respond to ego behaviour and whether protocols are symmetric between human and AV arms. The paper needs a prespecified construct definition for ordinary interaction quality, external human ratings or validated comfort/effort measures, and a clear causal diagram separating monitor inputs, contemporaneous state and outcomes. The authors must explain counterpart control policies and demonstrate that the outcome channels are behaviourally responsive. Otherwise, Fig. 5 should be presented as a kinematic association, not a validation layer.

7. **[Location: Methods 4.6; Fig. 6] — The human-driver study is not reportable or auditable in its present form.**  
   The manuscript states only that 20 licensed drivers completed 15 scenarios. It omits recruitment, inclusion/exclusion criteria, demographics and driving experience, country/site, compensation, informed consent, ethics/IRB approval, safety procedures, trial order and counterbalancing, familiarisation, attrition, missing runs, repeated-run policy, scenario randomisation, driver-level exclusions, and sample-size justification. Panel 6a explicitly omits uncertainty, and panel 6c reports 15/15 scenarios above parity without showing uncertainty or accounting for repeated observations within 20 drivers and 19 automated systems. These omissions are material, not administrative, because the human arm anchors the paper's strongest population comparison. The full protocol and ethics statement must be provided; the analysis should use the crossed repeated-measures structure, report driver- and scenario-level distributions and uncertainty, and state whether any endpoint, threshold, exclusion or scenario label was inspected before freezing the analysis.

8. **[Location: Introduction; Sections 2.3–2.6; Methods note on scope and Algorithms 1–2] — The paper does not demonstrate a runtime monitor or its value over simpler monitors.**  
   “Online” is defined as non-anticipating replay, while end-to-end real-time execution on vehicle hardware is explicitly not evaluated. No wall-clock latency, compute requirement, solver timeout distribution, memory use, sensor/map availability, tracking error, delayed/missing data behaviour, sequential false-alarm rate, persistence rule, or planner-interface experiment is reported. Algorithm 2 includes a separately calibrated persistence mechanism and planner-facing signal, but neither calibration nor results are given. In parallel, there is no comparison with simple risk-conditioned reference intervals, kinematic anomaly detectors, surprise/expectation models, learned trajectory likelihood, TTC-based monitors, or existing social-driving scores. Without those comparisons, it is unclear whether inverse-planning IPV contributes information beyond current geometry and kinematics. For an NMI paper centred on an “online monitor,” the authors should provide a causal streaming implementation benchmark and an ablation against credible alternative monitors under identical splits and abstention budgets. A closed-loop planner study is not strictly necessary for an auditing paper, but the current planner-interface language should then be removed or clearly quarantined as future work.

9. **[Location: Introduction; Sections 2.2–2.3; Fig. 3] — The novelty and generality relative to prior social-preference and anomaly-monitoring work are not demonstrated empirically.**  
   The components—trajectory-based SVO/IPV inference, conditional quantile modelling, conformal intervals, rejection/abstention and runtime monitoring—are individually established. The paper's novelty is their integration into a conditional social-atypicality pipeline, but the manuscript mostly distinguishes prior work verbally. Figure 3c shows that the full state description has zero or negative held-out-source \(R^2\), which limits the claim that a reusable state-conditioned surface has been discovered. The authors should define the precise methodological advance over their earlier IPV work and over learned-behaviour/surprise monitors, and evaluate whether the proposed monitor improves independent social-validity endpoints, calibration-efficiency trade-offs or transfer at a fixed abstention rate. Without this, the paper risks being a carefully packaged application of known elements rather than a broad machine-intelligence advance.

## 3. Minor issues

1. The abstract's opening statement that AVs “are certified for collision safety” is too general and should be replaced with a precise claim about existing safety evaluation or constraints.
2. The title and repeated “social compliance” phrasing exceed the explicitly supported estimand, conditional human atypicality. Either add normative validation or retitle around social-atypicality monitoring.
3. Give the complete mathematical definition of IPV, including the individual and group costs, their scaling and the mapping from \(\theta\) to reward weights; Eq. 1 alone does not define the construct.
4. Explain why the candidate grid excludes the endpoints of the stated parameter domain and what “100% of the admissible range” means in Fig. 4.
5. Define every element and threshold in \(o_{ij}\), \(q_a\), \(c_a\), “online-computable risk proxies,” “causal interaction progress,” “principal counterpart” and “frozen situation cells.”
6. Reconcile the reference-pool count of 2,442,625 anchor rows, the fold totals that sum to the same number, and the readability accounting over 4,497,368 anchor rows.
7. Reconcile 19 systems × 15 scenarios (285 possible runs) with 267 observed runs, 231 runs with a verdict, 175 non-scripted outcome runs, 120 flagged runs and the panel-specific sample counts in one participant/run flow diagram.
8. Report the full abstention-reason distribution in the benchmark and human arm, by scenario and system/driver. The statement that only human-support availability limits benchmark abstention appears inconsistent with the reported 55.3% readability rate and 14,099/67,861 verdict rate.
9. In Fig. 4, provide uncertainty for interval widths, coverage errors and explained variance. Large frame counts should not substitute for cluster-aware intervals.
10. In Fig. 5b, plot the estimate and interval for the <3 s threshold instead of encoding a null result as an absent mark. Do the same consistently for unsupported quantities.
11. State how multiplicity is handled across coverage levels, both sides of the interval, multiple nested emergency thresholds and the consequence battery, even if the battery was frozen.
12. Clarify whether “n” denotes moments, records, cases, scenes, runs, systems or drivers in every panel. Several figures mix these units.
13. Report frame rates, interpolation and temporal alignment across all four naturalistic sources and the real-vehicle datasets; a ten-frame window is not comparable unless preprocessing is explicit.
14. Describe how route-conditioned map geometry is available at decision time and test sensitivity to wrong route assignment, lane ambiguity and map error.
15. Explain the negative held-out-source \(R^2\) definition in Fig. 3c, the baseline used for zero, and uncertainty across cases; point estimates alone are inadequate.
16. Report the preregistration identifier, timestamp, frozen endpoints and deviations. Saying analyses were frozen or preregistered is not independently auditable without this information.
17. The data/code statements defer all derived data and implementation until publication. For review, the editor should obtain sufficient confidential artefacts to reproduce the central tables and figures, subject to benchmark restrictions.
18. Separate the descriptive worked examples in Figs. 1, ED1 and ED2 from evidential panels even more clearly in the Results; ED2 is one of only two runs satisfying highly selective display criteria.

## 4. Questions to the authors

1. What exact objective generates each candidate trajectory, and how do its self-progress and group-cost terms map to the stated IPV interpretation?
2. What independent evidence, in this dataset or prior work, establishes that the estimated IPV tracks perceived social preference rather than trajectory-fit residual? Does that evidence hold frame by frame and across datasets?
3. Can a simple model using only the current relative kinematics, risk proxies and interaction phase reproduce the flag assignments and every Fig. 5 association? What is IPV's incremental contribution?
4. What is the independent sampling unit for the claimed conformal guarantee? How is exchangeability justified when hundreds of calibration/test moments can come from one scene?
5. What are coverage and abstention jointly for every source and prespecified state subgroup? Why do Waymo and Lyft remain at approximately 74–75% coverage under a nominal 90% interval?
6. Was the support threshold tuned to coverage on any source later used for evaluation? How does the coverage–abstention curve change as that threshold varies?
7. How many contiguous assertive-side episodes do the 472 Fig. 5 moments represent, and do the conclusions persist with one index moment per episode/run?
8. What variables define the “frozen situation cells,” how many cells are sparse or lack overlap, and what balance remains after adjustment?
9. What were the numerical results of the whole-sequence placebo test? Were lead outcomes or pre-verdict trends tested as negative controls?
10. How are the 19 systems and 15 scenarios represented in inference? Do results persist under leave-one-system-out and leave-one-scenario-out analyses?
11. Which counterpart runs are scripted versus behaviourally responsive in the AV and human arms? How can counterpart speed reduction validate interaction response if the relevant counterpart controller is scripted?
12. What formal hypothesis was tested for “no alarm inflation” in the human arm, what equivalence margin was chosen in advance, and what are the driver- and scenario-clustered confidence intervals?
13. Were the 20 human drivers sampled from the same country/population as any reference source? What ethics approval, consent, safety protocol, trial order and exclusion rules applied?
14. Why should a 4.7% outside rate at a nominal 10% level be interpreted as successful transfer rather than substantial overcoverage and loss of sensitivity?
15. What is the end-to-end runtime per frame on representative hardware, including candidate generation, tracking, support search, interval inference and persistence logic?
16. How is the warning-persistence rule calibrated, and what is the per-interaction false-warning probability under human reference driving?
17. What aspect of the result would falsify the proposed social interpretation, rather than merely cause the monitor to abstain?

## 5. Prioritised revision requests

1. **Establish construct validity.** Add an independent, adequately powered human-evaluation or controlled-behaviour study linking IPV/flags to perceived assertiveness, accommodation and acceptability; compare against kinematic and learned-anomaly baselines.
2. **Reanalyse calibration with the correct dependence unit.** Use scene-/interaction-aware conformal calibration, report pointwise and episode-wise error, and give cluster-aware conditional coverage plus abstention across source and state groups.
3. **Reanalyse the consequence benchmark.** Define flag episodes, avoid overlapping-window pseudo-replication, show pretrends and placebo results, adjust transparently for phase/context, and model crossed system, scenario and run structure.
4. **Complete and properly analyse the human arm.** Supply ethics and participant/protocol details, cluster-aware uncertainty, driver/scenario distributions, equivalence hypotheses, missing-run accounting and symmetric endpoint definitions.
5. **Provide a reproducible estimator/reference specification.** Define the IPV objective, candidate generator, all features/gates/models/hyperparameters and sensitivity analyses, and reconcile all sample counts.
6. **Demonstrate online feasibility and added value.** Report streaming latency and failure modes on representative hardware, sequential warning calibration, and performance relative to credible alternative monitors at matched coverage/abstention budgets.
7. **Narrow the claims.** Unless normative validation is successful, replace “social compliance,” “interaction quality” and “interaction-benign” claims with conditional human-atypicality and kinematic-association language; state source-transfer failures prominently in the abstract and Discussion.
8. **Make the evidence auditable.** Provide the preregistration record, full inferential outputs, uncertainty in all central panels, a complete sample-flow diagram and review-accessible code/derived data sufficient to reproduce the main claims.
